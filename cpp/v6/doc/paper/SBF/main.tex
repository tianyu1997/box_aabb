for Multi-Query Manipulator Planning}

\begin{abstract}
Repeated manipulator-planning workloads often keep the robot kinematics fixed
while changing only task endpoints or workspace obstacles. In such settings,
collision certification can dominate the cost of answering multiple related
queries. \textsc{SafeBoxForest} (SBF) is a conservative multi-query framework
that builds volumetric corridors from axis-aligned boxes in configuration
space. Its central data structure, the Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT),
stores kinematics-keyed mappings from joint-space boxes to conservative
workspace envelopes. This separation allows expensive geometric evidence,
especially grid-style envelopes, to be reused across obstacle scenes and
processes, while inexpensive interval-AABB envelopes may still be recomputed
online when cache lookup and storage overhead are not justified. For each obstacle scene, SBF
validates free boxes, grows an adaptive RRT-like forest, exports a
face-adjacency corridor graph, and answers queries by box localization and
local path refinement. Under matched resource and timing accounting on the
typical shelf+IIWA workload, SBF reduces reusable build time by more than two orders
of magnitude relative to Drake IRIS+GCS, while maintaining similar online query
latency and producing the longer paths expected from conservative box
corridors. The same representation supports cross-scene replay of cached
envelopes and localized invalidation after obstacle insertion.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Motion planning, multi-query planning, box covers, interval arithmetic, envelope caching.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Motion planning for high-degree-of-freedom (DOF) manipulators remains difficult
when many related queries must be answered in static or slowly changing
environments. Sampling-based planners are widely used because they rely on
general collision predicates and scale well in high-dimensional spaces
\cite{lavalle2006planning,lavalle1998rapidly,kavraki1996probabilistic,
karaman2011sampling}. Their reusable structures, however, are usually
zero-volume point-and-edge graphs, such as PRM roadmaps or RRT trees. In
multi-query settings this can leave substantial repeated work in collision
checking and local graph expansion. Region-based planners and
Graph-of-Convex-Sets~(GCS) methods take a complementary approach by planning
over certified free-space regions~\cite{deits2015computing,
marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}. Their performance is then strongly
coupled to the cost and quality of the constructed regions. The systems-level
question is therefore how to accumulate, certify, and reuse geometric evidence
about free space across related queries.

This question is especially relevant in warehouse and shared-cell manipulation,
where the robot model is fixed while obstacles and task endpoints change. A
convex-region pipeline such as IRIS usually constructs scene-local regions for
the current obstacle layout. By contrast, the kinematic map from a bounded
joint interval to a conservative workspace envelope is independent of the
obstacles. This independence suggests separating reusable kinematic evidence
from scene-specific collision filtering.

\textsc{SafeBoxForest} (SBF) realizes this separation through the Lifelong
Envelope Cache Tree (LECT), a kinematics-keyed hierarchy over joint-space
boxes. LECT stores conservative endpoint and link-envelope evidence that can
be reused across processes and obstacle scenes for a fixed robot model. The
cache is selective: simple IFK-based AABB envelopes may be recomputed online,
whereas expensive payloads such as rasterized grid envelopes benefit from
persistence and reuse across scene changes despite their larger storage
footprint.

For a given obstacle set $\mathcal{O}$, SBF validates a subset of LECT boxes as
free, grows an obstacle-specific forest of conservative configuration-space
boxes, and exports a face-adjacency graph. Queries are answered by localizing
the endpoints in the box forest, searching the graph, and applying local
corridor-preserving refinement. Certificate claims refer only to this
conservative box-corridor object.

The design targets repeated-query workloads rather than single-query
asymptotic optimality or globally tight convex decomposition. The relevant
metrics are reusable build time, online query time, path quality, robustness to
scene variation, and the cost of updating certificates after scene edits.

\textbf{The main contributions of this paper are as follows:}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Interval-envelope certification for volumetric C-space planning.}
  We formulate conservative link envelopes from interval kinematics to derive a
  practical approximate cell decomposition in C-space.

  \item \emph{A scene-independent Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT).}
  We introduce a persistent, kinematics-keyed cache mapping C-space boxes to
  workspace envelopes, with particular benefit for expensive grid-style
  envelope payloads and cross-scene reuse for a fixed robot architecture.

  \item \emph{An adaptive RRT-like free-box forest with reusable querying.}
  We introduce the construction of a face-adjacent conservative box forest,
  enabling fast online querying through box localization, graph search, and
  lightweight path refinement.

  \item \emph{Reproducible evaluation under a fixed resource protocol.}
  We release the experiment drivers behind our generated tables. The reported
  benchmarks enforce matched seeds, pinned thread and CPU-core affinities,
  non-overlapping execution runs, and explicit timers separating build from
  query phases (\cref{sec:exp_repro}).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
\Cref{sec:link_envelopes} develops conservative link envelopes and endpoint
sources; \cref{sec:lect} describes LECT structure and persistence;
\cref{sec:forest} presents conservative forest construction, consolidation, and
corridor querying; \cref{sec:exp} reports experiments; and
\cref{sec:discussion,sec:conclusion} summarize limitations and closing remarks.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Convex Region Planning and Graphs of Convex Sets}

Convex region planners approach motion planning by constructing a certified cover
of free space and then solving graph search or convex optimization on top of
that cover. IRIS introduced the now-standard idea of computing large
collision-free convex regions by alternating separating hyperplanes and region
inflation~\cite{deits2015computing}. Later work extended the same line into
configuration space, improved region quality, or improved graph compactness
through visibility-graph and clique-cover constructions~\cite{amice2022finding,
petersen2023growing,werner2024approximating}. Marcucci \etal{} made the
Graph-of-Convex-Sets formulation particularly influential by showing that motion
planning can be cast as optimization over a graph of certified convex
regions~\cite{marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}.

SBF is aligned with this family in spirit but not in geometry: it instantiates
certified free-space regions as axis-aligned boxes in $C$-space rather than
general polytopes, trading per-region expressivity for lower build overhead and
simpler adjacency bookkeeping.
The induced cover is generally coarser than a high-quality convex
decomposition, but it avoids repeated hyperplane-based region inflation. SBF
therefore prioritizes reusable volumetric throughput and cacheable evidence
over the tightest possible per-scene convexification.

\subsection{Conservative Envelopes, Interval Methods, and Collision Checking}

Interval analysis provides a natural foundation for conservative geometric
certification because nonlinear image sets can be enclosed without losing
soundness~\cite{moore2009introduction,jaulin2001applied}. In robotics, interval
methods have long been used for kinematics and certified workspace
analysis~\cite{merlet2004solving,merlet2009interval}. SBF follows that
tradition, but shifts the unit of certification from a workspace reachability
set or a single configuration to a reusable configuration-space box. The key
object is therefore not a precise workspace mesh or a narrow-phase contact
query, but a conservative link envelope that can be cached and replayed across
many planning calls.

Classical collision libraries solve a different problem. Hierarchies such as
OBB trees and libraries such as FCL are effective for single-configuration
collision detection and proximity queries~\cite{gottschalk1996obbtree,
pan2012fcl,gilbert1988fast}. They do not, however, provide a scene-independent
cache of conservative interval-level evidence. SBF is complementary to this stack:
it uses conservative envelopes to avoid repeated narrow-phase work on already
validated boxes and uses standard discrete collision checks only for validation
outside the box-certificate claim.

\subsection{Multi-Query Reuse and Configuration-Space Decomposition}

Multi-query planners typically amortize effort by reusing a graph structure such
as a roadmap. PRM is the classical example: build a connectivity graph once and
reuse it for many start-goal pairs~\cite{kavraki1996probabilistic,
bohlin2000path}. Later lazy-search and certificate-based variants reduce online
edge evaluation or cache known-free configurations~\cite{dellin2016unifying,
schmerling2015optimal}. Experience-based systems likewise reuse prior search
results, usually in the form of trajectories or local connections rather than a
certified region decomposition~\cite{coleman2014moveit}.

SBF operates at a different reuse level. It caches not only paths or binary
collision outcomes at discrete configurations, but conservative geometric
evidence that an entire configuration-space box is free for a given obstacle
scene. PRM-like methods reuse zero-volume connectivity and must revalidate
affected edges after scene changes. Convex-region planners reuse higher-quality
regions, but those regions are usually tied to the obstacle layout in which
they were constructed. SBF instead uses conservative interval certification and
stores the kinematics-to-envelope mapping in a scene-independent LECT, then
applies scene-specific obstacle filtering on top of that evidence
\cite{lozanoperez1983spatial,brooks1985subdivision}. The trade-off is explicit:
SBF accepts axis-aligned boxes and conservative over-approximation in exchange
for simple validation, adjacency, and reuse across related queries.

\subsection{Sampling-Based Planning and Trajectory Optimization}

Sampling-based planners remain standard baselines when the goal is to solve
a single query quickly. RRT-Connect is particularly effective in manipulator
planning because it exploits bidirectional growth and often succeeds with very
little global structure~\cite{kuffner2000rrt}. Asymptotically optimal and
informed variants such as RRT*, BIT*, and FMT* improve path quality and search
efficiency when more time is available~\cite{karaman2011sampling,
gammell2015batch,gammell2018informed,janson2015fmt}. OMPL has made these
methods easy to deploy and benchmark in a common software stack~\cite{sucan2012ompl,
moll2015benchmarking}. However, standard RRT and its extensions produce single-use
evidence: the searched tree has zero volume and is typically discarded after one
query, requiring a full search from scratch for subsequent planning tasks in
the same scene.

By contrast, SBF uses adaptive RRT-like expansion to sample and certify boxes
from the LECT, producing a volumetric corridor structure that can be queried
repeatedly within the same environment.
Trajectory optimizers address a different stage of the pipeline. CHOMP, STOMP,
TrajOpt, and Gaussian-process motion planners refine an initial path by local
descent or sequential convex optimization~\cite{ratliff2009chomp,
kalakrishnan2011stomp,schulman2014motion,mukadam2018gpmp2}. SBF uses these
methods as a point of comparison rather than as direct substitutes: its aim is
to provide a reusable volumetric backbone on which graph search and local
post-processing can operate.

\textbf{Summary relative to \sbf.}
Convex-region methods and sampling-based planners cache complementary objects:
large per-scene polytopes or zero-volume graph edges and trees.
Certified-interval methods bound workspace sets, but they are rarely organized
as a persistent, kinematics-keyed volumetric decomposition for manipulator
planning. \textsc{SafeBoxForest} targets this setting by caching conservative
joint-box envelope evidence independently of obstacle scenes and then layering
scene-specific labeling and corridor adjacency on top (\cref{sec:lect,sec:forest}).

\section{Link Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:link_envelopes}

This section defines the link-envelope layer used by \sbf{}. The goal is to
outer-bound the workspace volume swept by a manipulator link over a joint box
$I \subseteq \Q$ using a compact certificate. The construction reduces link
bounding to two steps: bound the trajectories of a finite set of link endpoints,
then apply a constant-radius lift for capsule-like geometry. This reduction is
exact for ideal capsule links and remains conservative for broader convex
outer-envelope models.

Throughout the paper, $\Q$ denotes the joint-limit box explored by \sbf{}, and
workspace obstacles occupy $\mathcal{O}\subset\Real^3$. A joint interval
$I\subseteq\Q$ is certified free when every link envelope over $I$ is disjoint
from $\mathcal{O}$. The constructions below are inclusion-monotone: they may
reject feasible narrow-clearance boxes because of over-approximation, but they
do not certify a colliding box as free.

\subsection{Geometric Foundation}\label{sec:rewrite_geom}

Let $T(q)x = R(q)x + t(q)$ denote the rigid transform induced by a
configuration $q \in I$. For a reference body $C_0 \subset \Real^3$, its pose at
$q$ is $C(q)=T(q)C_0$. Two reductions are needed throughout the section. The
first is morphological: separate a constant-radius offset from its seed
geometry. The second is convex-geometric: replace a moving convex link by the
trajectories of a finite generating set.

\begin{definition}[Swept Workspace Envelope and Convex Envelope]
For a compact rigid reference body $C_0$, its exact swept workspace envelope
over interval $I$ is
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C_0) := \bigcup_{q\in I} C(q),
\]
and its convex envelope over $I$ is
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(C_0) := \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{S}_I(C_0)).
\]
\end{definition}

The swept envelope is the exact occupied workspace set of the moving body; the
convex envelope is the smallest convex set containing it. The latter is the
natural target when the downstream representation is convex, such as an AABB or
convex hull.

\begin{definition}[Morphological erosion and dilation]
Let $B_2(r)$ denote the closed Euclidean ball of radius $r$. For a compact set
$C \subset \Real^3$, define
\[
C \ominus B_2(r) := \{x \in \Real^3 : x + B_2(r) \subseteq C\},
\]
\[
C \oplus B_2(r) := \{x+y : x\in C,\ y\in B_2(r)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

It is important to separate exact offset identities from conservative
approximations. For a general non-smooth set, the opening
$(C \ominus B_2(r)) \oplus B_2(r)$ need not recover $C$: sharp intersections
can be rounded and narrow features can be removed. Thus, ``erode and dilate
back'' is not a universal identity for arbitrary convex polyhedra.

\begin{definition}[$r$-Offset Bounding Body]
A compact convex volume $C$ is an $r$-offset body if there exists a compact
convex seed set $S$ such that
\[
C = S \oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{definition}

Capsules are the main example used in this work: each capsule is the Minkowski
sum of a line segment and a Euclidean ball, so the radius can be handled
analytically.

\begin{lemma}[Analytical Radius Lift for Offset Bodies]\label{lem:offset_lift}
Let $C = S \oplus B_2(r)$ define an $r$-offset body. For any joint interval $I$,
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C) = \mathcal{S}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r),
\qquad
\mathcal{C}_I(C) = \mathcal{C}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
Consequently, if $\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S)$ is any conservative envelope with
$\mathcal{S}_I(S) \subseteq \widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S)$, then
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C) \subseteq \widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Rigid transforms commute with Euclidean ball offsets because rotations preserve
Euclidean distance. Thus, for each $q \in I$,
\[
T(q)(S \oplus B_2(r)) = T(q)S \oplus B_2(r).
\]
Taking the union over $q$ yields
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C)
= \begin{aligned}[t]
&\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(T(q)S \oplus B_2(r)\bigr) \\
=\;&\left(\bigcup_{q\in I} T(q)S\right) \oplus B_2(r) \\
=\;&\mathcal{S}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $B_2(r)$ is fixed and convex, convexification commutes with adding this
ball, proving the second identity. The final inclusion follows by monotonicity
of Minkowski addition.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Capsule modeling]\label{rem:capsule_exact}
The identity in \Cref{lem:offset_lift} is exact for capsules and other rounded
offset bodies. It is not exact for a sharp-cornered polytope unless that
polytope is itself represented as an offset body. This is why the evaluated
implementation uses conservative capsule models for robot links: the swept
volume can be bounded by a lower-dimensional swept seed, followed by an
inexpensive constant-radius Minkowski lift.
\end{remark}

The following result formalizes the endpoint reduction: tracking a finite
generating set is sufficient to outer-bound the swept convex envelope of a
polytope.

\begin{theorem}[Endpoint-envelope lifting for convex polytopes]
\label{thm:vertex_lift}
Let
\[
P = \operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_m\}
\]
be a convex polytope in its reference frame. For each vertex $v_i$, define the
trajectory set
\[
\Gamma_i(I) := \{T(q)v_i : q\in I\}.
\]
Let $\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ be any convex outer envelope of $\Gamma_i(I)$, i.e.,
$\Gamma_i(I) \subseteq \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ and
$\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ is convex. Then
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(P)
\subseteq
\operatorname{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)\right).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(P)
= \operatorname{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m \Gamma_i(I)\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $q\in I$. Rigid transforms preserve convex combinations, so
\[
T(q)P
= \begin{aligned}[t]
&T(q)\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_m\} \\
=\;&\operatorname{conv}\{T(q)v_1,\dots,T(q)v_m\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence every point $x \in T(q)P$ admits a representation
\[
x = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i T(q)v_i,
\qquad \lambda_i\ge 0,
\quad \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i = 1.
\]
Because $T(q)v_i \in \Gamma_i(I) \subseteq \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ for every $i$,
$x$ lies in $\operatorname{conv}(\cup_i \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I))$. This proves the
first claim. For the second, choose
$\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)=\Gamma_i(I)$ and then observe that each endpoint
trajectory point already belongs to the swept polytope of $P$, so the reverse
inclusion is immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Extension beyond polytopes]\label{rem:general_convex}
The same argument extends geometrically to any compact convex body $C$ by
replacing the finite vertex set with the set of extreme points
$\operatorname{ext}(C)$. However, that extension is usually not a finite
computational recipe because $\operatorname{ext}(C)$ may be infinite. In
practice, a finite reduction is most useful for two classes of shapes: convex
polytopes with a small number of vertices, and rounded offset bodies such as
capsules, where \Cref{lem:offset_lift} removes the radius analytically.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Capsule envelope from endpoint envelopes]
\label{cor:capsule_lift}
Let a capsule be written as $K=[a,b]\oplus B_2(r)$, where $[a,b]$ is its center
segment. Let $\widehat{\Gamma}_a(I)$ and $\widehat{\Gamma}_b(I)$ be convex outer
envelopes of the two endpoint trajectories. Then a conservative convex envelope
of the swept capsule is
\[
\operatorname{conv}\bigl(\widehat{\Gamma}_a(I) \cup
\widehat{\Gamma}_b(I)\bigr) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
If the exact endpoint trajectory sets are used, then the exact convex envelope
of the swept capsule is
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(K)
= \operatorname{conv}\bigl(\Gamma_a(I) \cup \Gamma_b(I)\bigr)
\oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{corollary}

This reduction motivates the capsule-link model used in the remainder of the
paper. Many manipulator links admit conservative capsule approximations; under
this model, certification reduces to two moving endpoints and one scalar
radius.

\subsection{Endpoint Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:rewrite_endpoint_env}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:linkiaabb} illustrates the AABB extraction pipeline.
Endpoint \iAABB{}s are computed by interval forward kinematics (IFK), combined
into a conservative zero-radius centerline envelope, and padded by the link
radius before collision querying. The remaining numerical task is therefore to
outer-bound each endpoint trajectory over $I$ by a reusable set $E_k(I)$.
Endpoint envelopes are the common interface between kinematic propagation and
the downstream link-envelope representations.

\begin{definition}[Endpoint Interval Envelope]\label{def:endpoint_env}
Let $\bm{p}_k(q) \in \Real^3$ be the workspace position of endpoint $k$ at
configuration $q$. Its exact trajectory set over a joint interval $I$ is
\[
\Gamma_k(I) := \{\bm{p}_k(q) : q\in I\}.
\]
An endpoint interval envelope is any bounded set $E_k(I)$ satisfying
$\Gamma_k(I) \subseteq E_k(I)$. The default \sbf{} implementation uses the
axis-aligned bounding box (AABB)
\[
E_k(I) := \operatorname{bbox}(\Gamma_k(I))
\]
as the baseline endpoint envelope.
\end{definition}

The AABB choice is a modeling decision rather than a theoretical necessity.
Other convex endpoint envelopes could be used without changing the logic of the
pipeline. We adopt AABBs because they regularize storage, support efficient
coordinate-wise min/max propagation, and compose directly with LinkIAABB and
its tighter descendants.

\paragraph{Interval forward kinematics (IFK).}
The default conservative source is interval forward kinematics (IFK), optionally
augmented with affine forms to reduce dependency loss. Given a joint box
\[
I = [q_1^-,q_1^+] \times \cdots \times [q_n^-,q_n^+],
\]
let $[\bm{T}_j](I_j)$ denote the interval DH transform of joint $j$ over its
local interval. The chain is then propagated recursively as
\[
[\bm{T}^{0:k}] = [\bm{T}^{0:k-1}] \otimes [\bm{T}_k](I_k),
\]
and the translational columns of $[\bm{T}^{0:k}]$ provide conservative
coordinate ranges for endpoint $k$. Because interval arithmetic is
inclusion-monotone, the resulting endpoint AABB is conservative by
construction: over-approximation may create false rejection, but never false
acceptance. This source is the conservative default because it is cheap enough for
the planning hot path and naturally compatible with tree refinement. When a box
is split, both children inherit most transform prefixes from the parent, so the
cost of recomputation is largely confined to the suffix of the chain. Its main
advantage is conservative certification with microsecond-scale throughput. Its main
weakness is the wrapping effect: repeated interval products accumulate
dependency loss, and the envelope becomes visibly loose on wide boxes or long
kinematic chains.

\paragraph{Incremental FK state reuse.}
The online implementation carries a transient FK state during tree descent. This
state stores the interval-valued prefix transforms
$[\bm{T}^{0:0}],\ldots,[\bm{T}^{0:n}]$ together with the per-joint DH interval
matrices used to produce them. At the root, or after restarting from an
arbitrary stored node, the full prefix chain is computed once. Thereafter, when
a split changes only joint interval $I_k$, all prefixes before $k$ are still
valid; the descent updates the single-joint matrix for $k$ and recomputes only
the suffix $[\bm{T}^{0:k+1}],\ldots,[\bm{T}^{0:n}]$. Thus the local update cost
is proportional to the remaining chain length rather than to a fresh full-chain
evaluation. This is not a persistent FK cache: it is a per-descent working state
used to materialize endpoint evidence cheaply before LECT decides whether that
evidence should be stored or replayed.

\paragraph{Critical-sample envelopes.}
The second source estimates the endpoint AABB from a set of critical or
near-critical samples on the interval boundary and interior. The guiding idea is
that coordinate extrema often occur near a small number of geometrically
meaningful joint assignments. A typical construction defines, for each joint,
the candidate set
\[
C_j(I) = \{q_j^-, q_j^+\} \cup \{k\pi/2 \in (q_j^-, q_j^+)\},
\]
then evaluates scalar forward kinematics on the Cartesian product of these
per-joint critical sets, optionally augmented with a few midpoint or
near-boundary probes. The method is motivated by the fact that many endpoint
coordinates attain extrema at interval boundaries or near turning points of
$\sin$ and $\cos$, so a small structured sample set can capture much of the
useful geometry missed by IFK. Its advantage is a favorable time-tightness
trade-off: the candidate transforms can be precomputed cheaply, while the
resulting envelopes are often much tighter than raw interval propagation. Its
limitation is equally important. A separable per-joint candidate set does not
guarantee completeness when the true extremum depends on coupled motion of
multiple joints, so CritSample is best interpreted as an advisory or profiling
source unless it is paired with an external certification step.

\paragraph{Analytical extrema.}
The third source solves endpoint-coordinate extrema from the trigonometric
structure of the kinematic chain itself. Because each revolute-joint transform
is affine in $(\cos q_j,\sin q_j)$, an endpoint coordinate reduces to a simple
sinusoid when only one joint is free and to a low-degree bilinear
trigonometric polynomial when two joints remain free. This yields a structured
multi-stage analytical solver: vertices and 1D boundary edges can be enumerated
exactly, 2D faces can be reduced to polynomial root finding, and
higher-dimensional residual cases can be handled by local descent together with
interval verification or branch-and-bound closure.

The analytical source occupies the tightest end of the source spectrum. Its
main advantage is envelope quality: when the stationary conditions are resolved
accurately, the endpoint envelope can approach the true extremal box much more
closely than IFK or CritSample. Its main drawback is resource cost. Analytical
solving is much more expensive than hot-path interval propagation, so
it is best viewed as an offline certification and profiling tool rather than as
the default source for online planning.

Taken together, these endpoint sources define a clear operating spectrum. IFK
is the most reliable conservative default for the planning hot path; CritSample is
the cheapest way to obtain tighter advisory envelopes; and the analytical
source offers the tightest endpoint envelopes at higher cost.
This motivates endpoint envelopes as the intermediate abstraction: the
downstream link-envelope layer remains unchanged while the upstream source is
chosen according to the certification and runtime budget of the application.

\subsection{Link Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:rewrite_link_env}

Once endpoint envelopes are available, the remaining design question is how to
represent the swept link volume compactly for collision refutation and optional
reuse. For link $\ell$ connecting endpoints $\bm{p}_{\ell-1}(q)$ and
$\bm{p}_{\ell}(q)$ with capsule radius $r_\ell$, the zero-radius center segment
is
\[
L_\ell(q) := \{(1-\tau)\bm{p}_{\ell-1}(q) + \tau \bm{p}_{\ell}(q) :
\tau \in [0,1]\}.
\]
The swept capsule over interval $I$ is
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(L_\ell(q) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)\bigr).
\]
By \Cref{lem:offset_lift}, \sbf{} first bounds the zero-radius centerline and
then applies the radius lift downstream.

\begin{definition}[Link Interval Envelope]\label{def:link_interval_env}
A conservative link interval envelope for link $\ell$ over interval $I$ is any
set $B_\ell(I)$ satisfying
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(L_\ell(q) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)\bigr) \subseteq B_\ell(I).
\]
We separate the zero-radius centerline bound from the final scalar padding:
\[
B_\ell(I) = B_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus \mathcal{R}(r_\ell),
\]
where $B_\ell^{\circ}(I)$ bounds the uninflated centerline and
$\mathcal{R}(r_\ell)$ is the chosen radius-padding operation. In the default
AABB implementation, $\mathcal{R}(r_\ell)=B_\infty(r_\ell)$, i.e., conservative
coordinate-wise padding.
\end{definition}

The following representations share the same endpoint evidence but preserve
different amounts of downstream geometric detail.

\paragraph{AABB envelope.}
An AABB envelope may be tightened by subdividing the reference center segment
into $S$ pieces. If
\[
s_j = a + \frac{j}{S}(b-a), \qquad j=0,\dots,S,
\]
then the $j$th subsegment $[s_{j-1},s_j]$ has its own centerline envelope
$B^{\circ}_{\ell,j}(I)$. The final representation is the union
\[
B^{(S)}_\ell(I) := \bigcup_{j=1}^{S}
\bigl(B^{\circ}_{\ell,j}(I) \oplus B_\infty(r_\ell)\bigr).
\]
When $S=1$, this reduces to the ordinary LinkIAABB representation
\[
B_\ell(I) = \operatorname{bbox}\bigl(E_{\ell-1}(I) \cup E_\ell(I)\bigr)
\oplus B_\infty(r_\ell).
\]
Larger $S$ values preserve more of the directional structure of the swept
segment because different subsegments can adapt to different local motion
ranges. The resulting representation is still attractive computationally:
once endpoint envelopes are available, each subdivided component can be
reconstructed by cheap interpolation and coordinate-wise padding, so tighter
AABB envelopes are well suited to on-demand evaluation. The key caveat is that
the subdivided structure must be preserved. If all sub-envelopes are collapsed
back into one global AABB, then most of the tightness gain disappears.


\paragraph{Hull envelope.}
When the endpoint envelopes are convex, a more geometry-aware zero-radius link
envelope is given by
\[
H_\ell^{\circ}(I) := \operatorname{conv}\bigl(E_{\ell-1}(I) \cup E_\ell(I)\bigr).
\]
The padded hull envelope is then $H_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)$, or its
coordinate-wise AABB surrogate
$H_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus B_\infty(r_\ell)$ in a purely box-based
implementation. Compared with a single AABB, the hull retains much more of the
directional structure of the swept segment and is therefore a natural bridge to
grid-based shape representations.

\paragraph{Grid envelope.}
Grid envelopes keep the same geometric input as the AABB or hull families but
replace a small collection of continuous boxes by a discrete outer
approximation on a voxel lattice. In the active paper pipeline we retain only
the hull-aware HullGrid variant, which rasterizes a tighter swept shape into
the grid representation. This is attractive when the planner needs a
shape-aware union operator rather than only a compact bounding box, but it is
not used as the default. The trade-off is the usual one: smaller
voxels produce tighter envelopes and more faithful unions, but increase
rasterization and storage cost. Because the main questions are then payload
layout, persistence, compression, and reuse across depth, the storage design of
grid envelopes is discussed together with LECT in the next section.

Thus, the link-envelope layer is separate from the upstream kinematics solver.
Once endpoint envelopes are available, the remaining design choice is the
representation. This separation allows LECT to remain kinematics-keyed and
scene-independent.

\section{Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree}\label{sec:lect}

The preceding section establishes the main invariant used by \sbf{}: link
interval envelopes depend on the robot kinematics and on the queried joint
box, but not on the current obstacle layout. The Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree
(LECT) organizes this scene-independent evidence in a persistent hierarchy.
LECT is not a uniform mesh of $\Q$, which would be infeasible in high dimension.
Instead, it materializes boxes only where downstream planning needs geometric
evidence and refines them only when tighter certificates are useful. Its value
is representation-dependent: inexpensive IFK+AABB envelopes may be cheaper to
compute online than to read from persistent storage, whereas rasterized grid
envelopes and union-refined payloads can benefit from persistence.

\subsection{Design Principles}\label{sec:lect_design}

LECT is a dynamically grown binary KD tree over the joint-limit box $\Q$. Each
node $v$ represents a joint interval $I_v \subseteq \Q$, with the root equal to
the full joint-limit box. The key design principle is that stored geometric
payloads are \emph{kinematics-keyed} rather than \emph{scene-keyed}. Once a
payload has been computed for a robot model and joint interval, the same
payload can be tested against different obstacle sets by changing only the
downstream obstacle-intersection query. This separates persistent geometric
evidence from mutable scene-level planning state.

\begin{definition}[LECT node]
A LECT node $v$ stores four pieces of information: (i) the joint interval
$I_v$ represented by the node; (ii) the split and symmetry metadata that relate
$v$ to its parent and children in the KD tree; (iii) cached endpoint-envelope
data from which link envelopes and parent refinements can be derived; and
(iv) occupancy and residency metadata that distinguish geometric certificate
state from planner-side box usage.
\end{definition}

The hierarchy is essential because no finite cache can enumerate joint
intervals densely in a high-dimensional space. Coarse nodes provide broad
coverage, while finer descendants are generated only where additional
resolution is needed. Endpoint AABBs are a common primitive for multiple
link-envelope representations. For simple AABB envelopes, however, the
implementation may recompute them online instead of forcing a cache read. Link
envelopes are materialized lazily from endpoint records, and per-link radii are
applied at collision-query time. The remaining subsections describe the split
strategy, parent refinement, and storage design.

\subsection{Best-Tighten KD Tree}
\label{sec:lect_split}

LECT effectiveness depends on where the tree is split. A useful split should
reduce downstream envelope slack, because tighter envelopes improve collision
refutation and reduce unnecessary refinement. For a parent interval $I$, let
$I_d^{-}$ and $I_d^{+}$ denote the two children obtained by splitting joint
dimension $d$ at its midpoint. Define the cumulative envelope score
\[
\mathcal{V}(I) := \sum_{\ell} \operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_\ell(I)\bigr),
\]
where $B_\ell(I)$ is the link-envelope representation used for link $\ell$.
The best-tighten criterion selects the split dimension that maximizes the
post-split reduction
\[
\Delta(d) := \mathcal{V}(I)
- \Bigl(\mathcal{V}(I_d^{-}) + \mathcal{V}(I_d^{+})\Bigr).
\]
Equivalently, it minimizes the post-split envelope sum, or a robust minimax
variant of that sum, so that refinement is directed toward dimensions that
contribute most to envelope looseness.

In practice, the basic objective is best enforced by probing the candidate
children and selecting the dimension with the smallest post-split envelope
score. A convenient robust form is the minimax criterion
\[
d^* = \arg\min_d
\max\!\left\{\sum_{\ell}\operatorname{vol}(B_\ell(I_d^-)),
\sum_{\ell}\operatorname{vol}(B_\ell(I_d^+))\right\},
\]
which avoids splits that tighten one child while leaving the other
nearly unchanged. For highly anisotropic intervals, this criterion can be
augmented with a width-aware penalty so that repeatedly splitting already
narrow dimensions is discouraged. To keep this optimization affordable, LECT
adopts a depth-synchronous split schedule: once the best dimension $d_h$ has
been selected for depth $h$, every node at that same depth is split along
$d_h$. This amortizes split probing, regularizes worker partitions, and keeps
the tree geometrically coherent across subproblems. The resulting KD tree is
therefore more than a geometric partition of $\Q$: it is a
certificate-aware refinement tree whose topology is chosen to reduce downstream
envelope slack under the split-probing budget.

\subsection{Parent-Envelope Refinement Across Depth}
\label{sec:lect_parent_refine}

Directly computing an envelope on a large interval is often loose. After a node
has been split, its two children carry strictly more local information, so the
parent's logical envelope can be refined using child information instead of
remaining tied to the original coarse certificate. This is the second
multiresolution principle behind LECT: refinement should improve not only the
children themselves, but also the representation of the region they came from.

\begin{proposition}[Child-union parent refinement]
\label{prop:parent_union}
Let a parent interval satisfy $I = I_L \cup I_R$, where $I_L$ and $I_R$ are the
two child intervals created by a binary split. If $B_\ell^L$ and $B_\ell^R$ are
conservative link envelopes for link $\ell$ over $I_L$ and $I_R$, respectively,
then
\[
\widetilde{B}_\ell(I) := B_\ell^L \cup B_\ell^R
\]
is a conservative envelope for link $\ell$ over the full parent interval $I$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The swept link set over the parent interval decomposes as
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} L_\ell(q)
= \left(\bigcup_{q\in I_L} L_\ell(q)\right)
\cup
\left(\bigcup_{q\in I_R} L_\ell(q)\right).
\]
By assumption, the first term is contained in $B_\ell^L$ and the second in
$B_\ell^R$. Their union therefore contains the full swept set over $I$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:parent_union} gives the parent-update rule: once a node has
been split, the union of child envelopes is a sound refined representation of
the parent. The retained benefit depends on the representation.
If the parent is allowed to store a union-like object, such as a sub-box family
or a sparse grid, the refinement is retained. In an AABB-based cache, however,
the most storage-efficient parent payload is usually the enclosing AABB of the
two child AABBs, which is compact but inevitably reintroduces some geometric
inflation. In a grid-based cache, by contrast, parent refinement is the
set-theoretic union of the two child voxel maps, implemented as brickwise OR
with no further shape loss beyond the voxelisation itself.

The design implication is representation-specific: AABB payloads prioritize a
compact persistent witness, while grid payloads preserve the multiscale union
structure. A parent node may keep a coarse index envelope for traversal, but
its geometric witness should be the union-refined payload inherited from its
children whenever the chosen representation supports that operation.
Refinement then improves not only leaf resolution, but also the persistent
geometric quality of previously visited coarse regions.

\subsection{Storage Structure and Persistence}\label{sec:lect_storage}

At the systems level, LECT is best organized as a flat node pool indexed by a
global node id space. Parallel arrays store interval bounds, child links,
canonical symmetry keys, endpoint-envelope payloads, optional persistent
grid-link payloads, parent-refinement payloads, occupancy counters, and
residency flags. This layout provides
$\mathcal{O}(1)$ random access, contiguous scans over envelope arrays, and a
clean separation between immutable geometric evidence and mutable planner-side
state. It also supports cheap worker snapshots: local trees can share the same
historical geometric base while adding only their newly materialized frontier,
after which newly created nodes are transplanted back into the master cache.
The storage policy follows reconstruction cost. Endpoint envelopes are the
common record for multiple downstream representations, but simple IFK+AABB
payloads are often cheap enough to recompute online. Thus, persistence is used
selectively rather than assumed to be faster for every envelope type. Grid link
envelopes are primary persistent payloads because voxelisation, brick
construction, and grid unions are comparatively expensive and can be reused
across depth. By contrast, subdivided AABB envelopes need not be stored as
first-class payloads: once endpoint evidence is available, an AABB envelope for
any subdivision level $S$ can be reconstructed quickly by interpolation and
coordinate-wise padding. The persistent layer therefore records endpoint
iAABBs, derived link envelopes, and optional grid payloads keyed by kinematic
intervals; the incremental FK state described above remains a transient online
state and is not serialized as a cache object.

\paragraph{Grid payload layout.}
Grid link envelopes are stored as sparse voxel bitmasks. In the
current design, workspace voxels are grouped into $8^3=512$-voxel BitBricks, so
each non-empty brick occupies 64 bytes and is indexed by its integer brick
coordinate. A grid envelope payload is therefore a sparse map from brick index
to brick word array. Collision checking reduces to wordwise bitwise-AND against
a pre-rasterized obstacle grid, while parent refinement and sibling merges are
implemented as brickwise bitwise-OR. This representation is especially valuable
inside LECT because the union of child grids is itself the exact parent payload
at the chosen voxel resolution, so no extra inflation is introduced during
multiresolution refinement beyond the voxelisation error already present.

\paragraph{AABB-gated grid refinement.}
Grid payloads are used as refinement evidence rather than as standalone
free-space certificates. For a node $v$, LECT first materializes the endpoint
\iAABB{}s and the derived padded link AABBs. Scene validation then performs a
link-AABB overlap test against the obstacle set; if no link AABB overlaps an
obstacle, the node is accepted without touching the grid cache. Only when the
AABB test leaves a possible collision and a grid envelope is configured does
the implementation materialize the pending grid payload. A cache hit reads the
sparse voxel grid lazily from the V6 grid cache, while a miss computes the grid
from the already available endpoint evidence and queues it for asynchronous
insertion. The fine test is then the wordwise grid--obstacle-grid overlap. This
ordering avoids loading voxel payloads for nodes eliminated by the AABB coarse
test while preserving soundness: grid evidence can refine an AABB collision
candidate, but it never replaces the endpoint/link-AABB evidence for the node.

\paragraph{Symmetry compression.}
For manipulators whose base joint exhibits a four-sector rotational symmetry, a
node key can be canonicalized to one representative $q_0$ sector and the stored
envelope rotated back to the queried sector at access time. Such a $Z_4$
symmetry layer is structurally compatible with LECT because the cache key is
already an interval in joint space, and it reduces duplicate storage for
repeatedly visited symmetric regions. One refinement is to align splits
along $q_0$ with the nearest sector boundary $k\pi/2$ rather than with the raw
geometric midpoint, so that neighbouring nodes map to the same canonical cache
sector whenever possible. Symmetry then acts as both a compression mechanism
and a split-shaping rule for cross-query reuse.

\paragraph{Persistence and residency management.}
LECT is intended to persist across independent processes, so its storage model
is a binary image with memory-mapped access, on-demand residency (lazy load),
and incremental flushing (incremental save). The node pool is split into a writable in-memory frontier
$V$ and a read-only mapped history $M$ identified by a robot-kinematics
fingerprint. Reads query $V$ first and then fall back to $M$; writes
materialize touched mapped nodes into $V$. On flush, newly added nodes are
appended in binary order, and the file header is updated through a crash-atomic
rename. Under this design, cold nodes remain on disk, hot nodes stay resident
in RAM, and later sessions can reuse previously materialized evidence. The
result is a cache designed for cross-scene and cross-process reuse, with the
largest amortization expected for expensive envelope payloads.

In what follows we treat \cref{sec:forest} as the consuming layer: given $I_v$, LECT
materializes kinematic envelopes and hierarchical refinement splits, while obstacle
checking, occupancy metadata, adjacency bookkeeping, and regrowth reactions to
changing $\mathcal{O}$ reside entirely inside the Safe Box Forest routines.

The previous section described the scene-independent certificate structure used
by \sbf{}. The role of this section is complementary: given a fixed obstacle
set $\calO$ and a set of task-relevant seeds, we construct an
obstacle-specific forest of conservative boxes together with the adjacency graph
used later for planning queries. The construction layer therefore consumes the
reusable evidence stored in LECT and turns it into a scene-level free-space
representation.

Throughout this section, a box-corridor certificate refers only to paths whose
waypoints and interpolated segments remain inside the validated box union. Any
local endpoint completion, smoothing, or discrete validation outside that union
is charged where used and is not counted as part of the conservative corridor
certificate.

Conceptually, the build process has three stages. First, a seed-guided descent
in LECT discovers conservative boxes around selected configurations. Second, these
boxes are expanded into a forest under a strict face-adjacency rule so that the
result remains graph-searchable as a corridor representation rather than only a
bag of disconnected conservative regions. Third, the forest is consolidated by
promotion and coarsening before it is exported as a face-adjacency graph. The
consolidation pass is part of the reusable build phase and its time is included
in reported build measurements. This section is organized around these stages.

\subsection{Construction Objective}\label{sec:forest_objective}

Let $\Q$ denote the joint-limit box and let $\calO$ denote the current obstacle
set. The construction objective is to build a finite family
\[
\mathcal{F} = \{B_1,\dots,B_N\}, \qquad B_i \subseteq \Q,
\]
such that every $B_i$ is a validated free-space box, the union
$\bigcup_i B_i$ covers as much useful query-relevant free space as possible
under the available box, time, and depth budgets, and the induced adjacency
graph supports efficient downstream search. This object is scene-specific:
changing $\calO$ changes which intervals are accepted into the forest, even
though the underlying envelope evidence in LECT remains reusable.

The central structural rule is that the forest is not merely a free-space cover,
but a \emph{corridor-compatible} free-space cover. Boxes are therefore inserted
under a face-adjacency constraint.

\begin{definition}[Face-adjacent boxes]\label{def:face_adjacent}
Let
\[
B_i = \prod_{d=1}^{n}[l_i^{(d)},u_i^{(d)}],
\qquad
B_j = \prod_{d=1}^{n}[l_j^{(d)},u_j^{(d)}].
\]
The boxes $B_i$ and $B_j$ are face-adjacent if there exists exactly one
dimension $d^\star$ such that the two intervals are flush in that dimension,
and the intervals overlap with positive measure in every other dimension. In
other words, the boxes share an $(n-1)$-dimensional face rather than only a
lower-dimensional boundary contact.
\end{definition}

This restriction is geometrically important. It excludes pinched contacts that
are formally connected as sets but unusable as stable corridor primitives. The
output of the construction stage is therefore a forest of conservative boxes whose
connectivity is already aligned with the path-planning role they must later
serve.

\subsection{Find Free Box}\label{sec:forest_ffb}

The most atomic construction primitive is the find-free-box procedure (pseudocode
labelled ``Find Free Box''). Given a seed configuration $q\in\Q$, this
procedure descends the LECT from the root toward the leaf interval that
contains $q$. Along this descent, the procedure carries the current joint
interval and its transient FK state; after each split, that FK state is updated
from the split dimension rather than recomputed from the root. Endpoint and
link envelopes are materialized lazily whenever they are needed, with node
payload access following the same lazy-load policy of \Cref{sec:lect_storage}
(read resident frontier first, then mapped history), and occupancy
metadata are consulted so that already saturated subtrees are skipped early. If
the current node is validated collision-free, it is returned immediately as a
box candidate; otherwise the node is refined along the depth-synchronous split
schedule of \Cref{sec:lect_split}, and the descent continues into the child
containing $q$.

The procedure therefore consumes the scene-independent cache in the way
it was designed to be used: the tree topology and endpoint payloads are reused,
while the scene-dependent question ``is this interval free under $\calO$?'' is
answered only when the grower actually visits that interval. FFB is also the
place where the two forms of laziness meet. Tree refinement is lazy because a
node is split only when a seed attempts to pass through it, and envelope
materialization is lazy because link-level payloads are instantiated only when
their collision-refutation value is needed. The corresponding construction
primitive is summarized in \Cref{alg:ffb_rewrite}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{FindFreeBox}---lazy LECT descent with conservative scene validation}
\label{alg:ffb_rewrite}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Seed configuration $q$, LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$, depth cap $D_{\max}$
\ENSURE Validated conservative interval $I_v$ or failure
\STATE $v \gets \mathrm{root}(T)$; $d \gets 0$; initialize current interval and FK state
\WHILE{$d \le D_{\max}$}
  \STATE \textit{// Cache materialization}
  \STATE materialize the endpoint and link envelopes of $v$ if needed
  \STATE \textit{// Scene validation}
  \IF{$v$ is marked fully occupied}
    \STATE \textbf{return} failure
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$v$ is validated collision-free with respect to $\calO$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} interval $I_v$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE \textit{// Refinement}
  \IF{$d = D_{\max}$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} failure
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$v$ has no children}
    \STATE split $v$ along the depth-synchronous split dimension of depth $d$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $k \gets$ split dimension of $v$
  \STATE $v \gets$ the child of $v$ whose interval contains $q$; update the current interval
  \STATE update the FK state from dimension $k$ onward; $d \gets d+1$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{return} failure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Termination and conservative output of the find-free-box procedure]
\label{prop:ffb_rewrite}
Let the LECT depth cap be $D_{\max}$. The find-free-box procedure terminates
after at most $D_{\max}+1$ descent or refinement decisions. Moreover, every
interval returned by the procedure is a conservative box, i.e., every
configuration in that interval is collision-free with respect to $\calO$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each step of the find-free-box procedure either returns immediately or moves
one level deeper into the tree, so the number of possible descent decisions
is bounded by the depth cap. If an interval is returned, its link envelopes
have been verified disjoint from every obstacle in $\calO$. By the envelope
construction of \Cref{sec:link_envelopes}, those envelopes conservatively
contain the swept link geometry over the whole interval, so every configuration
in the interval is collision-free.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Forest Growth}\label{sec:forest_growth}

Starting from an initial seed set, the forest is grown by repeatedly invoking
the find-free-box procedure on carefully chosen candidate configurations.
Each seed first produces a root box through the find-free-box procedure, and
these root boxes initialize one or more connected components of the forest.
The main grower should be understood as an
evidence-guided expansion process rather than as a generic random cover:
every iteration chooses a frontier component, proposes a nearby interval worth
testing, and accepts the result only when it extends the forest by a usable
face-adjacent corridor segment.

The two key decisions in each iteration are therefore where to expand and how
to turn that decision into a conservative box. A natural policy is coordinated
multi-tree growth: components that are still small or still disconnected
receive higher priority, because their expansion contributes most to global
corridor formation. Once a frontier box has been selected, a new candidate seed
is generated by sampling in configuration space, identifying the nearest
conservative box, and projecting the sample toward a boundary location that
  encourages face-adjacent expansion. The find-free-box procedure is then called on this projected seed.
  The candidate is accepted into the forest only if it is validated by the
  find-free-box procedure and preserves the face-adjacency structure of
  \Cref{def:face_adjacent}. When
components stall, the same loop shifts the sampling distribution toward
candidate connector regions so that later find-free-box calls concentrate near
missing corridor links. The resulting method is summarized in
\Cref{alg:forest_growth}.

In addition to uniform and goal-biased sampling, the grower uses an
``unexplored-region'' sampling mode. With probability $p_u$ (default
$\sbfScanUnexplored$ in the paper configuration) a candidate sample is drawn by
performing a free-volume-weighted walk down the current LECT: leaves with
larger unoccupied free volume are more likely to be selected. This biases FFB
toward under-explored parts of configuration space, improving coverage
efficiency and reducing the need for costly cross-component connector search. The
mechanism is compatible with both coordinated multi-tree growth and serial
RRT-like expansion.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{GrowForest}---budgeted face-adjacent conservative forest expansion}
\label{alg:forest_growth}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$, seed set $\mathcal{S}$, box budget $N_{\max}$, time budget $t_{\max}$
\ENSURE Conservative forest $\mathcal{F}$
\STATE $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset$; initialize connected components from $\mathcal{S}$
\FORALL{$q \in \mathcal{S}$}
  \STATE $B \gets \text{FindFreeBox}(q, T, \calO)$
  \IF{$B \neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE insert $B$ into $\mathcal{F}$ as a root box
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\WHILE{$|\mathcal{F}| < N_{\max}$ and time budget not exhausted}
  \STATE $C \gets \textsc{SelectFrontierComponent}(\mathcal{F})$
  \STATE $q_{\mathrm{cand}} \gets \textsc{SampleCandidate}(T,\mathcal{F})$
  \STATE $B_{\mathrm{near}} \gets \textsc{NearestFrontierBox}(q_{\mathrm{cand}},C)$
  \STATE $q_{\mathrm{seed}} \gets \textsc{ProjectToFace}(q_{\mathrm{cand}},B_{\mathrm{near}})$
  \STATE $B_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \text{FindFreeBox}(q_{\mathrm{seed}}, T, \calO)$
  \IF{$B_{\mathrm{new}} \neq \emptyset$ and $B_{\mathrm{new}}$ is face-adjacent to $B_{\mathrm{near}}$}
    \STATE \textsc{AcceptIfFaceAdjacent}$(B_{\mathrm{new}},B_{\mathrm{near}})$
    \STATE insert $B_{\mathrm{new}}$ into $\mathcal{F}$; \textsc{UpdateComponents}$(\mathcal{F})$
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \RETURN $\mathcal{F}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The current paper configuration uses coordinated multi-goal growth as the main
path. Five seed configurations initialize the shelf roots, and the grower uses
the fixed 8-core/8-thread budget specified in \cref{sec:exp_repro}. The default
keeps the growth schedule simple enough to preserve one authoritative forest
state, while LECT supplies reusable kinematic evidence across threads and
cache-warm reruns. An experimental partitioned LECT grower is available as an
engineering variant, but it is not the default used for the shelf+IIWA results
reported below. The output of this section is the conservative box-corridor
object only.

\subsection{Forest Consolidation}\label{sec:forest_consolidation}

Raw growth tends to produce a forest that is finer than necessary. The purpose
of consolidation is therefore to replace a large collection of locally valid
boxes by a smaller collection of equally conservative but more useful regions.
Two operations are central.

The first is \emph{promotion}. When two occupied sibling leaves are both
collision-free and their parent interval is also collision-free, the two leaves can be
replaced by the parent. This is the most local form of coarsening and directly
reuses the multiresolution structure of LECT.

The second is \emph{multi-level coarsening}. Exact face-contact merges are the
cleanest case: if two boxes share a full face and their union is itself one box,
the merged region remains conservative by construction. More general adjacency
merges are handled by constructing a candidate parent region and accepting it
only when its chosen envelope representation is still conservative against the
current obstacle set. This is where the representation choices of
\Cref{sec:link_envelopes,sec:lect} become operational. In an AABB-based forest, the compact parent witness
is typically the enclosing AABB of the two child boxes. In a grid-based forest,
the parent witness is the exact voxel union of the two child payloads. The
former is cheaper to store, while the latter preserves geometric detail more
faithfully across depth. The consolidation logic is summarized in
\Cref{alg:forest_consolidation}.
The final containment-pruning step removes only boxes that are fully covered by
accepted conservative regions, so it changes neither the represented free-space
union nor the certificate argument.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{ConsolidateForest}---certificate-preserving promotion, merge, and pruning}
\label{alg:forest_consolidation}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Conservative forest $\mathcal{F}$, LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$
\ENSURE Consolidated conservative forest $\mathcal{F}'$
\STATE $\mathcal{F}' \gets \mathcal{F}$
\STATE \textit{// Stage 1: sibling promotion}
\REPEAT
  \STATE promote any validated sibling leaves whose parent is collision-free
\UNTIL{no eligible sibling pair remains}
\STATE \textit{// Stage 2: conservative face/contact merging}
\STATE merge exact face-contact pairs whose union is itself a single box
\FORALL{adjacent candidate pairs $(B_i,B_j)$ in $\mathcal{F}'$}
  \STATE build a candidate parent region $H$ and score its volume expansion
  \IF{the chosen envelope representation of $H$ is conservatively collision-free with respect to $\calO$}
    \STATE replace $B_i,B_j$ by $H$
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{// Stage 3: containment pruning}
\STATE remove redundant boxes that are fully contained in larger conservative regions
\STATE \RETURN $\mathcal{F}'$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Sound forest consolidation]
\label{thm:forest_consolidation}
Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a forest of conservative boxes. Suppose that every promotion
or coarsening step replaces a set of boxes by a new region $H$ only when the
selected envelope representation of $H$ is conservatively collision-
free with respect to $\calO$. Then the resulting forest
$\mathcal{F}'$ is still a forest of conservative boxes and satisfies
\[
\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{F}} B \subseteq \bigcup_{B'\in\mathcal{F}'} B'.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every accepted parent or merged region contains the boxes it replaces, so the
union of the forest cannot shrink. By assumption, each replacement is accepted
only after a conservative envelope check has shown that the new region is
collision-free. Therefore every region in $\mathcal{F}'$ remains conservative.
\end{proof}

This theorem clarifies why consolidation belongs in the main method narrative:
the forest is not post-processed by an arbitrary geometry simplifier, but by a
second conservative construction stage whose accepted outputs remain valid
planning regions. Consolidation is therefore the certificate-preserving
reduction that turns a raw growth trace into a compact, query-ready corridor
structure.

\subsection{Corridor Query}
\label{sec:forest_graph}

After growth and consolidation, the forest is exported as a graph
$G=(V,E)$ with one vertex per conservative box. Two vertices are connected by an
edge when their boxes are face-adjacent in the sense of
\Cref{def:face_adjacent}. Lightweight querying is possible because this
face-adjacency structure is enforced during growth and consolidation rather
than repaired after graph export.

This pair $(\mathcal{F},G)$ is the final planning object produced by the build
stage. Querying it is intentionally lightweight. Given start and goal
configurations $q_s,q_g$, the planner associates them with start-side and
goal-side boxes in $\mathcal{F}$ and then searches $G$ for a connecting box
sequence. The query problem is therefore reduced to box-corridor
retrieval inside an already constructed free-space representation.

A shortest path on $G$ yields a box sequence whose union defines a conservative
corridor between the two query endpoints. A geometric path is then recovered as
a waypoint chain inside that corridor. The optional post-processing used in
experiments is limited to corridor-constrained waypoint elimination and local
refinement. This step is purely local; it introduces no new global planning
problem, and feasibility is inherited from the conservative corridor itself.

For dynamic scenes, the same explicit forest representation supports a local
certificate update that invalidates only boxes affected by an obstacle edit and
then repairs the surviving adjacency graph. When localized regrowth is enabled,
new boxes are accepted only after the same conservative envelope validation as
in the original build. The obstacle-add experiment uses this localized
invalidation and graph-repair routine.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fairness Protocol}

All experiments were executed on a workstation equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i5-12600KF processor and 32\,GB of RAM, running Ubuntu 20.04.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]

The second block moves downstream to link-envelope design. We return to the
same four width-stratified IIWA14 interval bins as above, with 400 boxes per
bin (1600 total), and measure total runtime for the endpoint-plus-envelope
pipeline. The main set uses CritSample in eight rows (LinkIAABB
$S=1,2,4,8$ and HullGrid $\delta=0.02,0.04,0.06,0.08$) and adds two IFK
control rows (IFK+LinkIAABB $S=4$ and IFK+HullGrid $\delta=0.04$).

First, we sweep the LinkIAABB subdivision count.
\Cref{tab:link_envelope_pipeline} reports link-envelope tightness, runtime,
and cache-footprint metrics. The CritSample rows show a clear but modest
benefit from subdivision: the volume ratio decreases from 100.0\% at $S=1$ to
85.9\% at $S=4$, while $t_{\mathrm{eval}}$ increases only from
9.06~$\mu$s to 9.70~$\mu$s. Increasing to $S=8$ further reduces the ratio to
83.9\%, but the additional gain is small. We therefore retain $S=4$ as the
non-grid default.

The second stage sweeps HullGrid across the voxel-size schedule. At
$\delta=0.04$ m, HullGrid reduces the volume ratio to 74.9\% with
$t_{\mathrm{eval}}=50.04~\mu$s and about 4.6k occupied voxels. The finer
$\delta=0.02$ m setting is tighter (61.3\%) but costs
134.0~$\mu$s and produces a much larger payload. Thus, moderate-resolution
HullGrid is useful when the planner benefits from a shape-aware raster payload,
but it is not the default replacement for LinkIAABB.



\Cref{tab:link_envelope_pipeline} also reports cache-side diagnostics from a
controlled shelf+IIWA replay. In every compared run, LECT starts empty, the
tree-snapshot cache is disabled, and the cold-fill row disables V6 cache
reads/probes while writing the EP/Grid entries later consumed by the validated
cache-hit replay. A cache-hit row is retained only when the build profile reports an
all-hit or near-all-hit EP/Grid replay, with no material grid recomputation.
These columns therefore measure
geometric-evidence reuse on a matched replay route rather than the much larger
effect of loading a pre-expanded tree.

In that table, $t_{\mathrm{eval}}$ denotes a standalone local microkernel time
in $\mu$s: the script measures endpoint-envelope evaluation, then the chosen
link-envelope construction, and reports their sum. This deliberately excludes
grower-side parent-FK reuse and route-level cache effects. The column
$t_{\mathrm{replay}}$ denotes grower-side cache-replay time per expansion call,
estimated from the cache-hit build profile as envelope-expansion time divided
by the number of expand calls. Unlike $t_{\mathrm{eval}}$,
$t_{\mathrm{replay}}$ is keyed only by envelope representation ($S$ and
$\delta$), not by endpoint source; the endpoint and grid hit counters are used
only to validate that the retained replay rows are all-hit or near-all-hit
cache replays.

These columns show why LECT should not be interpreted as a universal speedup
layer. For the IFK+LinkIAABB$_4$ control row, the standalone microkernel and
grower replay are in the same microsecond range
($t_{\mathrm{eval}}=1.24~\mu$s versus
$t_{\mathrm{replay}}=1.11~\mu$s), so the route-level results below determine
whether persistence is worthwhile for this simple envelope. By contrast, grid
envelopes have high construction costs and larger payloads; their value comes
from avoiding repeated voxelisation and preserving union-refined grid evidence
across depth and across runs. The D32 and disk columns therefore quantify the
cost of filling and storing persistent evidence, not a claim that every cache
replay is faster than recomputation.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The SafeBoxForest system embodies concrete trade-offs between conservative
coverage, kinematic caching, and planning latency. Below we summarize modeling
limitations, empirical interpretation of the benchmarks, engineering scope of the
reported configurations, and reproducibility notes.

\paragraph{Geometric and modeling scope.}
The theoretical reductions emphasize capsule-like links and convex outer bounds;
sharp polyhedral geometry without an offset representation inherits conservative
but potentially loose envelopes (\cref{sec:link_envelopes}). Axis-aligned
configuration-space boxes trade expressive region shape for cheap certification,
adjacency tests, and cache-friendly replay; they are not chosen to minimize
per-query path length against heavy nonconvex-region optimizers on a single
instance. These are deliberate design points rather than implementation gaps.

\paragraph{Cache and envelope scope.}
LECT is not a universal acceleration layer. The measured IFK+LinkIAABB case
shows that direct online envelope evaluation can be faster than a persisted
cache read for simple AABB payloads. LECT is most useful when the cached object
is expensive to reconstruct, as with HullGrid voxelisation and union-refined
grid payloads, or when the same kinematic evidence is reused across processes,
queries, and obstacle scenes. The cache-footprint measurements in
\cref{tab:marcucci_envelope_build} make this trade-off explicit: grid payloads
can deliver large reuse gains, but they also require hundreds of MB of
persistent storage, whereas simple AABB payloads may be better left on the
online path.

\paragraph{Conservatism and path quality.}
The conservative guarantees rely on interval endpoint bounds, axis-aligned
configuration-space boxes, and conservative coarsening. These choices can reject
feasible narrow-clearance boxes because of IFK dependency loss or box-level
over-approximation. They also bias the planner toward box-corridor paths rather
than single-query shortest paths. The reported longer SBF paths should therefore
be interpreted as the expected cost of prioritizing reusable certification and
low build latency over asymptotic path optimality.

\paragraph{Empirical generality.}
All cross-method comparisons are tied to the protocol in
\cref{sec:exp_repro}: fixed thread and core affinities, no concurrent
experiment groups, common seed sets, and explicit build versus query timers.
Generality to new robots, scene distributions, or collision engines is
supported only insofar as the same invariants (conservative envelopes, valid
obstacle models) hold. The UR5/Panda randomized family in
\cref{tab:panda,fig:exp5_cross_robot_baselines} is the paper's generalization
setting and supplies the obstacle-add rows in \cref{tab:exp6_rebuild}; this is
not a claim of domain independence. The held-out cache rows in
\cref{tab:marcucci_envelope_build} instead use a fixed-IIWA randomized-obstacle
protocol to isolate scene variation from robot-kinematics variation. Similarly,
LECT persistence is architected for cross-process reuse through a
robot-kinematics fingerprint and process-independent storage. This paper
measures both shelf+IIWA matched-route replay and fixed-IIWA held-out
cross-scene cache reuse for the retained payloads
(\cref{tab:marcucci_envelope_build}); larger deployment-scale cache traces with
long-lived job histories remain future work.

\paragraph{Seed sensitivity and initialization burden.}
Under the retained shelf+IIWA and UR5/Panda randomized-scene protocols in
\cref{tab:query,tab:panda}, IRIS-style pipelines are sensitive to the quality
and coverage of initial region seeds. In the randomized UR5 groups,
IRIS-NP reaches $20\%$, $4\%$, and $0\%$ SR from Easy to Hard, while the Panda
groups reach $76\%$, $68\%$, and $20\%$ for the same baseline. SBF is also seed-driven, but its
coordinated multi-seed expansion can continue from other frontier components
when a subset of seeds is weak. This is an empirical observation under the
reported protocols, not a guarantee across all scene families.

\paragraph{Build configuration scope.}
The reported shelf+IIWA and UR5/Panda randomized-scene results use a single documented SBF configuration (growth schedule, thread budget, and optional coarsening flags) as stated in \cref{sec:forest_growth,sec:exp_e2e}.
Changing connector search, consolidation passes, or partitioned grower layouts redistributes wall-clock across growth and merging; we omit a separate build-only ablation table in this version and instead treat such knobs as engineering variants of the same conservative pipeline.
Localized certificate repair after obstacle addition is isolated in \cref{tab:exp6_rebuild}.

\paragraph{Artifact and reproducibility.}
The accompanying implementation and numbered experiment drivers used to produce
the generated tables can be rerun under the same flags to regenerate timing and
aggregate statistics; configuration paths and aggregation scripts are written to
match the definitions above. The artifact package records the exact command-line
flags, generated-table paths, and aggregation scripts used for this submission.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \textsc{SafeBoxForest} (\sbf{}), a conservative
multi-query planning framework that combines interval-envelope certification,
a scene-independent Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT), and adaptive RRT-like
free-box growth. By decoupling robot-kinematics evidence from scene-specific
obstacle filtering, SBF can reuse certified geometric structure across repeated
queries and scene edits. On the typical shelf+IIWA workload ported
from~\cite{marcucci2023motion}, Drake IRIS+GCS and SBF are compared under the
matched resource protocol summarized in \cref{sec:exp_repro}; SBF lowers
reusable build cost by more than two orders of magnitude while online query
latency remains in the same range. Its paths are generally longer, consistent
with the box-corridor objective. On the UR5/Panda randomized-scene
generalization family (\cref{tab:panda}), the same qualitative build-time gap
remains, while path quality and success rate depend more strongly on topology
and seed coverage.

\sbf{} is best suited to long-horizon or batch manipulation regimes with stable
robot kinematics and repeated query structure. In this setting, LECT
persistence is most valuable for expensive envelope payloads such as grids and
for cross-scene reuse, but the measured footprint trade-off supports keeping
simple IFK+AABB envelopes on the online computation path. The limits of
axis-aligned covers, capsule-centric envelopes,
and workload-scoped evaluation are summarized in \Cref{sec:discussion}.

Future work will focus on improving residual connectivity in narrow passages, generalizing
cache and forest update rules to accommodate highly dynamic environments, and maintaining
certificates under incremental scene changes.
