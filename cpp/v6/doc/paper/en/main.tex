\title{SafeBoxForest:\\ Fast C-Space Box Covers and Lifelong Envelope Caching
for Multi-Query Manipulator Planning}

\author{TIAN,~Yu and Ren,~Hongliang%
\thanks{The authors are with the Department of Electronic Engineering,
The Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK), Hong Kong SAR, China.
E-mail: \texttt{yutian@link.cuhk.edu.hk}.}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Repeated high-DOF manipulator queries are often dominated by collision checking.
We present \textsc{SafeBoxForest} (SBF), a conservative multi-query planning framework
that combines interval-envelope certification with reusable box-corridor search.
Its core data structure, the Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT), persistently
caches the mapping from C-space boxes to workspace envelopes and is keyed only
by robot kinematics, not by obstacle scenes. This separation enables cross-scene
evidence reuse while preserving conservative collision guarantees.
On top of LECT, SBF grows an adaptive RRT-like forest of free C-space boxes,
builds a face-adjacency graph, and answers queries through box localization,
graph search, and lightweight refinement.
Under a matched protocol, on a shelf-scene workload ported from
\cite{marcucci2023motion}, one reusable SBF build is over two orders of magnitude
faster than IRIS while maintaining comparable online query latency, at the cost
of moderately longer paths. Additional experiments show stable endpoint/link-envelope
design choices, cache-warm replay gains, and cross-robot transfer behavior.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Motion planning, multi-query planning, box covers, interval arithmetic, envelope caching.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Motion planning for high-degree-of-freedom (DOF) manipulators remains challenging,
especially when many related queries must be answered in the same or slowly
changing environments. Sampling-based planners remain the practical default
because they require only a black-box collision predicate and scale well in
high-dimensional spaces~\cite{lavalle2006planning,lavalle1998rapidly,
kavraki1996probabilistic,karaman2011sampling}. However, both multi-query
roadmaps (e.g., PRM) and single-query trees (e.g., RRT) are built on
zero-volume point/edge structures. In repeated-query workloads, this often
shifts the dominant cost to repeated collision checking and local graph
expansion rather than reusable volumetric free-space structure.
Region-based planners and
Graph-of-Convex-Sets~(GCS) methods attack this bottleneck from the opposite
direction by moving more work offline and planning over certified free-space
regions~\cite{deits2015computing,marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}.
However, once region quality becomes the main determinant of performance, the
real systems question is no longer only how to search for one path, but how to
accumulate, certify, and reuse geometric evidence about free space across a
stream of related queries.

This issue is central in long-horizon manipulation settings (e.g., shelving,
pick-and-place, and shared workcells), where robot kinematics are fixed but
obstacles and endpoints evolve. Exact convex-region pipelines such as IRIS often
recompute geometric evidence after scene changes. Yet the key invariant is
kinematic: the mapping from a C-space interval to a conservative workspace
envelope depends on robot geometry, not on a particular obstacle set. LECT
isolates and persists this mapping. When scenes change, SBF invalidates only
affected boxes and regrows locally, preserving most previously certified evidence.

\textsc{SafeBoxForest} is naturally built around this decomposition. Its core data
structure is a Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree~(LECT), a kinematics-keyed cache of
endpoint and link envelopes over joint-space boxes. In this paper,
\emph{lifelong} is used in an operational sense: the cached geometric evidence
is persistent across processes and obstacle scenes and is invalidated only when
the robot kinematics change. The certification pipeline follows a simple chain:
interval forward kinematics maps a joint-space box to endpoint \iAABB s,
adjacent endpoint boxes define conservative link envelopes, and a configuration-
space box is accepted only when all of its link envelopes are disjoint from all
workspace obstacles. Crucially, the mapping from a joint-space interval to its
corresponding workspace envelope is fundamentally independent of obstacles,
depending solely on the robotic structure. This makes the cached object a true,
scene-independent, and lifelong data structure.

Built on top of LECT, SBF grows an obstacle-specific forest of conservative
configuration-space boxes, constructs a face-adjacency graph, and answers
queries by box localisation, graph search, and lightweight path refinement.
This design preserves a clean separation between reusable geometric evidence and
scene-level planning state. It also forces an important reporting discipline:
the box corridor and any point-level fallback are not conflated. In
the current code path, IFK-derived envelopes are the conservative hot path stored
in the main cache, while any point-level bridge used to repair a disconnected
corridor is treated as an end-to-end fallback and reported explicitly rather
than folded into the certificate.

The resulting objective differs from both one-shot samplers and exact convex-cover
optimizers. SBF does not target globally tightest regions per scene or
single-query optimality; it targets reusable conservative evidence with low
update and query cost. Accordingly, the key metrics are reusable-build cost,
cached online-query latency, robustness under scene variation, and retained
certificate utility after scene edits.

\textbf{The main contributions are as follows.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Interval-envelope certification for volumetric C-space planning.}
  We build conservative link envelopes from interval kinematics and obtain a
  practical approximate cell decomposition in C-space.

  \item \emph{A scene-independent Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT).}
  We introduce a persistent, kinematics-keyed cache from C-space boxes to
  workspace envelopes, enabling cross-scene evidence reuse for a fixed robot.

  \item \emph{An adaptive RRT-like free-box forest with reusable querying.}
  We construct a face-adjacent conservative box forest and support fast online
  querying through box localization, graph search, and lightweight refinement.

  \item \emph{A reproducible empirical study under one fixed protocol.}
  We report endpoint/link-envelope trade-offs, cache replay behavior, end-to-end
  baselines, cross-robot transfer, and build-stage ablations with matched seeds,
  thread/core budgets, and timing definitions.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
\Cref{sec:link_envelopes} develops conservative link envelopes and endpoint
sources; \cref{sec:lect} describes LECT structure and persistence;
\cref{sec:forest} presents conservative forest construction, consolidation, and
corridor querying; \cref{sec:exp} reports experiments; and
\cref{sec:discussion,sec:conclusion} summarise limitations and closing remarks.

In all, SBF is designed around one operating point: conservative volumetric
coverage with high certificate throughput, lifelong evidence reuse, and explicit
cross-query amortization under fixed kinematics.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Convex Region Planning and Graphs of Convex Sets}

Convex region planners attack motion planning by constructing a certified cover
of free space and then solving graph search or convex optimisation on top of
that cover. IRIS introduced the now-standard idea of computing large
collision-free convex regions by alternating separating hyperplanes and region
inflation~\cite{deits2015computing}. Later work extended the same line into
configuration space, improved region quality, or improved graph compactness
through visibility-graph and clique-cover constructions~\cite{amice2022finding,
petersen2023growing,werner2024approximating}. Marcucci \etal{} made the
Graph-of-Convex-Sets formulation particularly influential by showing that motion
planning can be cast as optimisation over a graph of certified convex
regions~\cite{marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}.

SBF is aligned with this family in spirit but not in geometry. Its free-space
regions are axis-aligned configuration-space boxes derived via geometry-inspired
methods, rather than general optimal polytopes. This choice intentionally trades
per-region topological complexity for a system that is more lightweight and
operationally flexible, with substantially lower build-time overhead. The resulting cover is coarser than a high-quality convex
decomposition, but avoids the heavy computational and memory constraints of
computing exact separating hyperplanes. SBF is accordingly best viewed as a more agile alternative that prioritises
reusable volumetric throughput and lifelong cache reuse over the geometrically
tighter but substantially more expensive convex decompositions produced by
solvers such as IRIS.

\subsection{Conservative Envelopes, Interval Methods, and Collision Checking}

Interval analysis provides a natural foundation for conservative geometric
certification because nonlinear image sets can be enclosed without losing
soundness~\cite{moore2009introduction,jaulin2001applied}. In robotics, interval
methods have long been used for kinematics and certified workspace
analysis~\cite{merlet2004solving,merlet2006interval}. SBF follows that
tradition, but shifts the unit of certification from a workspace reachability
set or a single configuration to a reusable configuration-space box. The key
object is therefore not a precise workspace mesh or a narrow-phase contact
query, but a conservative link envelope that can be cached and replayed across
many planning calls.

Classical collision libraries solve a different problem. Hierarchies such as
OBB trees and libraries such as FCL are effective for single-configuration
collision detection and proximity queries~\cite{gottschalk1996obbtree,
pan2012fcl,gilbert1988fast}. They do not, however, provide a scene-independent
cache of conservative interval-level evidence. SBF is complementary to this stack:
it uses conservative envelopes to avoid repeated narrow-phase work on already
validated boxes, and reserves point-level collision checking for the remaining
hard cases.

\subsection{Multi-Query Reuse and Configuration-Space Decomposition}

Multi-query planners typically amortise effort by reusing a graph structure such
as a roadmap. PRM is the classical example: build a connectivity graph once and
reuse it for many start-goal pairs~\cite{kavraki1996probabilistic,
bohlin2000path}. Later lazy-search and certificate-based variants reduce online
edge evaluation or cache known-free configurations~\cite{dellin2016unifying,
schmerling2015optimal}. Experience-based systems likewise reuse prior search
results, usually in the form of trajectories or local connections rather than a
certified region decomposition~\cite{coleman2014moveit}.

SBF operates at a different reuse layer. What is cached is not the path itself,
nor only a collision verdict at one configuration, but the geometric witness
that an entire configuration-space box is safe. In standard PRM-like multi-query
methods, the planner builds a roadmap that yields a zero-volume graph of points
and lines; if an obstacle shifts slightly, the roadmap requires fragile edge
re-checking. SBF intrinsically solves this by yielding a volumetric free-box
set, effectively turning approximate cell decomposition into a robust primitive.
The key difference is that SBF insists on conservative interval certification and
stores the C-space to workspace mapping in a persistent, scene-independent LECT,
rather than treating them as a throwaway discretisation~\cite{lozanoperez1983spatial,brooks1985subdivision}.
In short: PRM-style reuse caches zero-volume connectivity; convex-region planners cache complex per-scene polytopes; SBF caches conservative box intervals that are scene-independent and persistently reusable across queries.

\subsection{Sampling-Based Planning and Trajectory Optimisation}

Sampling-based planners remain the strongest baselines when the goal is to solve
a single query quickly. RRT-Connect is particularly effective in manipulator
planning because it exploits bidirectional growth and often succeeds with very
little global structure~\cite{kuffner2000rrt}. Asymptotically optimal and
informed variants such as RRT*, BIT*, and FMT* improve path quality and search
efficiency when more time is available~\cite{karaman2011sampling,
gammell2015batch,gammell2018informed,janson2015fmt}. OMPL has made these
methods easy to deploy and benchmark in a common software stack~\cite{sucan2012ompl,
moll2015benchmarking}. However, standard RRT and its extensions produce single-use
evidence: the searched tree has zero volume and is typically discarded after one
query, requiring a full search from scratch for subsequent planning tasks in
the same scene.

By contrast, SBF adopts an adaptive RRT-like expansion to efficiently sample and
certify boxes from the LECT, but crucially yields persistent volumetric corridors
that natively support instant multi-query planning within a given environment.
Trajectory optimisers address a different stage of the pipeline. CHOMP, STOMP,
TrajOpt, and Gaussian-process motion planners refine an initial path by local
descent or sequential convex optimisation~\cite{ratliff2009chomp,
kalakrishnan2011stomp,schulman2014motion,mukadam2018gpmp2}. SBF uses these
methods as a point of comparison rather than as direct substitutes: its aim is
to provide a reusable volumetric backbone on which fast graph search and light
post-processing can operate.

\section{Link Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:link_envelopes}

This section develops the geometric foundation behind the link-envelope layer
used by \sbf{}. The central question is how to outer-bound the swept workspace
volume of a rigid link over a joint interval $I \subseteq \Q$ using a small,
reusable certificate. Our answer is to reduce link envelopes to endpoint
envelopes and a radius-lift step. The reduction is exact for capsule links and
extends as a conservative convex-envelope argument to more general convex link
geometries. We first separate the constant-radius term for offset bodies such as
capsules, then show that the swept convex envelope of a convex link can be
controlled through the motion of a finite generating set, and finally
instantiate this reduction with the endpoint and link-envelope representations
used by the planner.

\subsection{Geometric Foundation}\label{sec:rewrite_geom}

Let $T(q)x = R(q)x + t(q)$ denote the rigid transform induced by a
configuration $q \in I$. For a reference body $C_0 \subset \Real^3$, its pose at
$q$ is $C(q)=T(q)C_0$. Two reductions are needed throughout the section. The
first is morphological: separate a constant-radius offset from its seed
geometry. The second is convex-geometric: replace a moving convex link by the
trajectories of a finite generating set.

\begin{definition}[Swept envelope and convex envelope]
For a compact reference body $C_0$, the swept envelope over $I$ is
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C_0) := \bigcup_{q\in I} C(q),
\]
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(C_0) := \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{S}_I(C_0)).
\]
\end{definition}

The swept envelope is the exact occupied workspace set of the moving body; the
convex envelope is the smallest convex set containing it. The latter is the
natural target when the downstream representation is convex, such as an AABB or
convex hull.

\begin{definition}[Morphological erosion and dilation]
Let $B_2(r)$ denote the closed Euclidean ball of radius $r$. For a compact set
$C \subset \Real^3$, define
\[
C \ominus B_2(r) := \{x \in \Real^3 : x + B_2(r) \subseteq C\},
\]
\[
C \oplus B_2(r) := \{x+y : x\in C,\ y\in B_2(r)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

For later use, it is important to separate the exact and inexact cases. In
general, the morphological opening $(C \ominus B_2(r)) \oplus B_2(r)$ is not
identically equal to $C$: sharp corners may be rounded off and narrow features
may disappear. Therefore, ``erode first, then dilate back'' is \emph{not} an
exact identity for an arbitrary convex body.

\begin{definition}[$r$-offset body]
A compact convex body $C$ is an $r$-offset body if there exists a compact convex
seed set $S$ such that
\[
C = S \oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{definition}

Capsules are the main example in this paper: each capsule is exactly the offset
of a line segment by a Euclidean ball. For such bodies, the radius can be
separated analytically.

\begin{lemma}[Radius lift for offset bodies]\label{lem:offset_lift}
Let $C = S \oplus B_2(r)$ be an $r$-offset body. Then, for any interval $I$,
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C) = \mathcal{S}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r),
\qquad
\mathcal{C}_I(C) = \mathcal{C}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
Consequently, if $\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S)$ is any conservative envelope with
$\mathcal{S}_I(S) \subseteq \widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S)$, then
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C) \subseteq \widehat{\mathcal{E}}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Rigid transforms commute with Euclidean ball offsets because rotations preserve
Euclidean distance. Thus, for each $q \in I$,
\[
T(q)(S \oplus B_2(r)) = T(q)S \oplus B_2(r).
\]
Taking the union over $q$ yields
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(C)
= \begin{aligned}[t]
&\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(T(q)S \oplus B_2(r)\bigr) \\
=\;&\left(\bigcup_{q\in I} T(q)S\right) \oplus B_2(r) \\
=\;&\mathcal{S}_I(S) \oplus B_2(r).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $B_2(r)$ is fixed and convex, convexification commutes with adding this
ball, proving the second identity. The final inclusion follows by monotonicity
of Minkowski addition.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why capsules matter]\label{rem:capsule_exact}
\Cref{lem:offset_lift} is exact for capsules and, more generally, for rounded
offset bodies. It is not exact for a sharp-cornered convex polytope unless that
polytope already admits such an offset representation. This is one reason the
capsule model is especially attractive in conservative planning: it turns a 3D
swept-volume problem into a swept-seed problem plus a constant-radius lift.
\end{remark}

The next result explains why endpoint envelopes are sufficient to outer-bound
the swept convex envelope of an entire convex link: it suffices to track a
finite generating set and recover the full convex envelope by convexification.

\begin{theorem}[Endpoint-envelope lifting for convex polytopes]
\label{thm:vertex_lift}
Let
\[
P = \operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_m\}
\]
be a convex polytope in its reference frame. For each vertex $v_i$, define the
trajectory set
\[
\Gamma_i(I) := \{T(q)v_i : q\in I\}.
\]
Let $\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ be any convex outer envelope of $\Gamma_i(I)$, i.e.,
$\Gamma_i(I) \subseteq \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ and
$\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ is convex. Then
\[
\mathcal{S}_I(P)
\subseteq
\operatorname{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)\right).
\]
Moreover,
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(P)
= \operatorname{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m \Gamma_i(I)\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $q\in I$. Rigid transforms preserve convex combinations, so
\[
T(q)P
= \begin{aligned}[t]
&T(q)\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_m\} \\
=\;&\operatorname{conv}\{T(q)v_1,\dots,T(q)v_m\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence every point $x \in T(q)P$ admits a representation
\[
x = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i T(q)v_i,
\qquad \lambda_i\ge 0,
\quad \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i = 1.
\]
Because $T(q)v_i \in \Gamma_i(I) \subseteq \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)$ for every $i$,
$x$ lies in $\operatorname{conv}(\cup_i \widehat{\Gamma}_i(I))$. This proves the
first claim. For the second, choose
$\widehat{\Gamma}_i(I)=\Gamma_i(I)$ and then observe that each endpoint
trajectory point already belongs to the swept polytope of $P$, so the reverse
inclusion is immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Extension beyond polytopes]\label{rem:general_convex}
The same argument extends geometrically to any compact convex body $C$ by
replacing the finite vertex set with the set of extreme points
$\operatorname{ext}(C)$. However, that extension is usually not a finite
computational recipe because $\operatorname{ext}(C)$ may be infinite. In
practice, a finite reduction is most useful for two classes of shapes: convex
polytopes with a small number of vertices, and rounded offset bodies such as
capsules, where \Cref{lem:offset_lift} removes the radius analytically.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Capsule envelope from endpoint envelopes]
\label{cor:capsule_lift}
Let a capsule be written as $K=[a,b]\oplus B_2(r)$, where $[a,b]$ is its centre
segment. Let $\widehat{\Gamma}_a(I)$ and $\widehat{\Gamma}_b(I)$ be convex outer
envelopes of the two endpoint trajectories. Then a conservative convex envelope
of the swept capsule is
\[
\operatorname{conv}\bigl(\widehat{\Gamma}_a(I) \cup
\widehat{\Gamma}_b(I)\bigr) \oplus B_2(r).
\]
If the exact endpoint trajectory sets are used, then the exact convex envelope
of the swept capsule is
\[
\mathcal{C}_I(K)
= \operatorname{conv}\bigl(\Gamma_a(I) \cup \Gamma_b(I)\bigr)
\oplus B_2(r).
\]
\end{corollary}

  This reduction motivates focusing on capsule links for the remainder of the
  paper: most industrial manipulator links admit a conservative capsule model,
  and once that model is adopted, the certification problem reduces to two
  moving endpoints plus one scalar radius.

\subsection{Endpoint Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:rewrite_endpoint_env}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:linkiaabb} illustrates the construction used by the
AABB pipeline. The left panel shows link~2's capsule and a sampled motion
trajectory; the middle panel visualises the endpoint \iAABB{}s computed from
interval forward kinematics; the right panel shows how the two endpoint boxes
combine into a conservative zero-radius link envelope which is then padded by
the link radius $r$ at collision-query time. This configuration is used in the
experiments to demonstrate the LinkIAABB construction and the scene-independent
radius-lift used by the planner.

The previous subsection reduces the link problem to endpoint motion. The
remaining numerical task is therefore to outer-bound each endpoint trajectory
over $I$ by a reusable set $E_k(I)$. In \sbf{}, endpoint envelopes are the
common intermediate between upstream kinematic solvers and downstream
link-envelope representations.

\begin{definition}[Endpoint interval envelope]\label{def:endpoint_env}
Let $\bm{p}_k(q) \in \Real^3$ denote the position of endpoint $k$ under
configuration $q$. Its exact trajectory set over interval $I$ is
\[
\Gamma_k(I) := \{\bm{p}_k(q) : q\in I\}.
\]
An endpoint interval envelope is any set $E_k(I)$ satisfying
$\Gamma_k(I) \subseteq E_k(I)$. In this paper we use the axis-aligned bounding
box
\[
E_k(I) := \operatorname{bbox}(\Gamma_k(I))
\]
as the default endpoint envelope.
\end{definition}

The AABB choice is a modelling decision rather than a theoretical necessity.
Other convex endpoint envelopes could be used without changing the logic of the
chapter. We adopt AABBs because they regularise storage, support fast
coordinate-wise min/max propagation, and compose directly with both LinkIAABB
and its tighter descendants.

\paragraph{Interval front kinematics (IFK).}
The default conservative source is interval front kinematics (IFK), optionally
augmented with affine forms to reduce dependency loss. Given a joint box
\[
I = [q_1^-,q_1^+] \times \cdots \times [q_n^-,q_n^+],
\]
let $[\bm{T}_j](I_j)$ denote the interval DH transform of joint $j$ over its
local interval. The chain is then propagated recursively as
\[
[\bm{T}^{0:k}] = [\bm{T}^{0:k-1}] \otimes [\bm{T}_k](I_k),
\]
and the translational columns of $[\bm{T}^{0:k}]$ provide conservative
coordinate ranges for endpoint $k$. Because interval arithmetic is
inclusion-monotone, the resulting endpoint AABB is conservative by
construction: over-approximation may create false rejection, but never false
acceptance. This source is the conservative default because it is cheap enough for
the planning hot path and naturally compatible with tree refinement. When a box
is split, both children inherit most transform prefixes from the parent, so the
cost of recomputation is largely confined to the suffix of the chain. Its main
advantage is therefore robustness and microsecond-scale throughput. Its main
weakness is the wrapping effect: repeated interval products accumulate
dependency loss, and the envelope becomes visibly loose on wide boxes or long
kinematic chains.

\paragraph{Critical-sample envelopes.}
The second source estimates the endpoint AABB from a set of critical or
near-critical samples on the interval boundary and interior. The guiding idea is
that coordinate extrema often occur near a small number of geometrically
meaningful joint assignments. A typical construction defines, for each joint,
the candidate set
\[
C_j(I) = \{q_j^-, q_j^+\} \cup \{k\pi/2 \in (q_j^-, q_j^+)\},
\]
then evaluates scalar forward kinematics on the Cartesian product of these
per-joint critical sets, optionally augmented with a few midpoint or
near-boundary probes. The method is motivated by the fact that many endpoint
coordinates attain extrema at interval boundaries or near turning points of
$\sin$ and $\cos$, so a small structured sample set can capture much of the
useful geometry missed by IFK. Its advantage is an excellent time-tightness
trade-off: the candidate transforms can be precomputed cheaply, while the
resulting envelopes are often much tighter than raw interval propagation. Its
limitation is equally important. A separable per-joint candidate set does not
guarantee completeness when the true extremum depends on coupled motion of
multiple joints, so CritSample is best interpreted as an advisory or profiling
source unless it is paired with an external certification step.

\paragraph{Analytical extrema.}
The third source solves endpoint-coordinate extrema from the trigonometric
structure of the kinematic chain itself. Because each revolute-joint transform
is affine in $(\cos q_j,\sin q_j)$, an endpoint coordinate reduces to a simple
sinusoid when only one joint is free and to a low-degree bilinear
trigonometric polynomial when two joints remain free. This yields a natural
multi-stage analytical solver: vertices and 1D boundary edges can be enumerated
exactly, 2D faces can be reduced to polynomial root finding, and
higher-dimensional residual cases can be handled by local descent together with
interval verification or branch-and-bound closure.

The analytical source occupies the tightest end of the source spectrum. Its
main advantage is envelope quality: when the stationary conditions are resolved
accurately, the endpoint envelope can approach the true extremal box much more
closely than IFK or CritSample. Its main drawback is resource cost. Analytical
solving is substantially more expensive than hot-path interval propagation, so
it is best viewed as an offline certification and profiling tool rather than as
the default source for online planning.

Taken together, these endpoint sources define a clear operating spectrum. IFK
is the most reliable conservative default for the planning hot path; CritSample is
the cheapest way to obtain tighter advisory envelopes; and the analytical
source offers the tightest endpoint envelopes at substantially higher cost.
This spectrum is exactly why the planner is
structured around endpoint envelopes as an intermediate abstraction: the
downstream link-envelope layer stays unchanged while the upstream source is
chosen according to the certification and runtime budget of the application.

\subsection{Link Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:rewrite_link_env}

Once endpoint envelopes are available, the remaining design question is no
longer how to run forward kinematics, but how to represent the swept link in a
form that is cheap to store, cheap to query, and sufficiently tight for
collision refutation. Let link $\ell$ connect endpoints
$\bm{p}_{\ell-1}(q)$ and $\bm{p}_{\ell}(q)$, and let its capsule radius be
$r_\ell$. The centre segment at configuration $q$ is
\[
L_\ell(q) := \{(1-\tau)\bm{p}_{\ell-1}(q) + \tau \bm{p}_{\ell}(q) :
\tau \in [0,1]\}.
\]
The exact swept link volume is
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(L_\ell(q) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)\bigr).
\]
By \Cref{lem:offset_lift}, it is sufficient to construct a conservative
envelope for the swept centre segment and then apply a constant-radius lift.

\begin{definition}[Link interval envelope]\label{def:link_interval_env}
A link interval envelope for link $\ell$ over interval $I$ is any set
$B_\ell(I)$ satisfying
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} \bigl(L_\ell(q) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)\bigr) \subseteq B_\ell(I).
\]
In the implementation, it is convenient to separate the zero-radius centreline
envelope from the final padding:
\[
B_\ell(I) = B_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus \mathcal{R}(r_\ell),
\]
where $B_\ell^{\circ}(I)$ is the centreline envelope and
$\mathcal{R}(r_\ell)$ is the chosen radius model. In the AABB pipeline,
$\mathcal{R}(r_\ell)=B_\infty(r_\ell)$ because coordinate-wise padding is the
cheapest conservative implementation.
\end{definition}

The remainder of the subsection is therefore best read as a representation
spectrum: all constructions share the same endpoint evidence, but they preserve
different amounts of geometric detail downstream.

\paragraph{AABB envelope.}
An AABB envelope may be tightened by subdividing the reference centre segment
into $S$ pieces. If
\[
s_j = a + \frac{j}{S}(b-a), \qquad j=0,\dots,S,
\]
then the $j$th subsegment $[s_{j-1},s_j]$ has its own centreline envelope
$B^{\circ}_{\ell,j}(I)$. The final representation is the union
\[
B^{(S)}_\ell(I) := \bigcup_{j=1}^{S}
\bigl(B^{\circ}_{\ell,j}(I) \oplus B_\infty(r_\ell)\bigr).
\]
When $S=1$, this reduces to the ordinary LinkIAABB representation
\[
B_\ell(I) = \operatorname{bbox}\bigl(E_{\ell-1}(I) \cup E_\ell(I)\bigr)
\oplus B_\infty(r_\ell).
\]
Larger $S$ values preserve more of the directional structure of the swept
segment because different subsegments can adapt to different local motion
ranges. The resulting representation is still attractive computationally:
once endpoint envelopes are available, each subdivided component can be
reconstructed by cheap interpolation and coordinate-wise padding, so tighter
AABB envelopes are well suited to on-demand evaluation. The key caveat is that
the subdivided structure must be preserved. If all sub-envelopes are collapsed
back into one global AABB, then most of the tightness gain disappears.


\paragraph{Hull envelope.}
When the endpoint envelopes are convex, a more geometry-aware zero-radius link
envelope is given by
\[
H_\ell^{\circ}(I) := \operatorname{conv}\bigl(E_{\ell-1}(I) \cup E_\ell(I)\bigr).
\]
The padded hull envelope is then $H_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus B_2(r_\ell)$, or its
coordinate-wise AABB surrogate
$H_\ell^{\circ}(I) \oplus B_\infty(r_\ell)$ in a purely box-based
implementation. Compared with a single AABB, the hull retains much more of the
directional structure of the swept segment and is therefore a natural bridge to
grid-based shape representations.

\paragraph{Grid envelope.}
Grid envelopes keep the same geometric input as the AABB or hull families but
replace a small collection of continuous boxes by a discrete outer
approximation on a voxel lattice. In the active paper pipeline we retain only
the hull-aware HullGrid variant, which rasterises a tighter swept shape into
the grid representation. This is attractive when the planner needs a
shape-aware union operator rather than only a compact bounding box, but it is
not treated as a blanket default. The trade-off is the usual one: smaller
voxels produce tighter envelopes and more faithful unions, but increase
rasterisation and storage cost. Because the main questions are then payload
layout, persistence, compression, and reuse across depth, the storage design of
grid envelopes is discussed together with LECT in the next section.

The outcome is that the link-envelope layer is conceptually separate from the
upstream kinematics solver. Once endpoint envelopes are available, the rest is a
representation problem. That separation is exactly what allows the LECT cache to
remain kinematics-keyed and scene-independent.

\section{Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree}\label{sec:lect}

The previous section established the key invariant used by \sbf{}: link
interval envelopes depend on robot kinematics and on the queried joint interval,
but not on the current obstacle layout. The Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree
(LECT) turns that observation into a reusable data structure. Its purpose is
not to cover all of $\Q$ densely in advance; that would be infeasible in a
7-DoF configuration space. Instead, it stores envelope evidence hierarchically,
so coarse nodes capture broad regions of $\Q$ and finer nodes are materialised
only where the planner actually needs tighter certificates.

\subsection{Design Principles}\label{sec:lect_design}

The LECT is a binary KD tree over the joint-limit box $\Q$. Each node $v$
represents a joint interval $I_v \subseteq \Q$, while the root corresponds to
the full joint-limit domain. Its organising principle is that cached envelopes
are \emph{kinematics-keyed} rather than \emph{scene-keyed}. Once interval-level
geometric evidence has been established for a robot model, the same witness can
be reused across obstacle layouts; only the obstacle-side collision query
changes. This separates reusable geometric computation from scene-specific
planning state and is what makes the cache genuinely multi-query.

\begin{definition}[LECT node]
A LECT node $v$ stores four pieces of information: (i) the joint interval
$I_v$ represented by the node; (ii) the split and symmetry metadata that relate
$v$ to its parent and children in the KD tree; (iii) cached endpoint-envelope
data from which link envelopes and parent refinements can be derived; and
(iv) occupancy and residency metadata that distinguish geometric certificate
state from planner-side box usage.
\end{definition}

The hierarchy is essential because no finite cache can enumerate arbitrary
intervals densely in a high-dimensional joint space. Coarse nodes provide broad
coverage, while finer descendants are generated only where additional geometric
resolution is needed. Endpoint AABBs are the natural persistent primitive,
because they can be reused by multiple downstream link-envelope
representations. Link envelopes are then materialised lazily from endpoint
records, and per-link radii are applied only at collision-query time. This
keeps the cached object compact, scene-independent, and representation-agnostic
while ensuring that a query traverses only the portion of the tree needed to
localise a seed or tighten a difficult certificate. The remaining subsections
describe the split strategy, parent refinement, and storage design that make
this possible.

\subsection{Best-Tighten KD Tree}
\label{sec:lect_split}

The quality of a LECT depends strongly on how intervals are split. A good split
is one that tightens the downstream link envelopes as much as possible, because
envelope tightness directly controls both collision-refutation power and cache
reuse quality. The split rule should therefore follow the geometry of envelope
slack rather than joint width alone.

Let $I$ be a node interval, and let $I_d^{-}$ and $I_d^{+}$ denote the two
children obtained by splitting $I$ at the midpoint of joint dimension $d$.
Define the aggregate envelope volume
\[
\mathcal{V}(I) := \sum_{\ell} \operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_\ell(I)\bigr),
\]
where $B_\ell(I)$ is the chosen link-envelope representation for link $\ell$.
A natural \emph{best-tighten} criterion is then to select the split dimension
that maximizes the post-split reduction
\[
\Delta(d) := \mathcal{V}(I)
- \Bigl(\mathcal{V}(I_d^{-}) + \mathcal{V}(I_d^{+})\Bigr).
\]
Equivalently, one may minimise the post-split sum
$\mathcal{V}(I_d^{-}) + \mathcal{V}(I_d^{+})$ or its minimax variant. All of
these formulations capture the same geometric objective: refine the dimension
whose uncertainty contributes most to envelope looseness.

In practice, the basic objective is best enforced by probing the candidate
children and selecting the dimension with the smallest post-split envelope
score. A convenient robust form is the minimax criterion
\[
d^* = \arg\min_d
\max\!\left\{\sum_{\ell}\operatorname{vol}(B_\ell(I_d^-)),
\sum_{\ell}\operatorname{vol}(B_\ell(I_d^+))\right\},
\]
which avoids pathological splits that tighten one child while leaving the other
nearly unchanged. For highly anisotropic intervals, this criterion can be
augmented with a width-aware penalty so that repeatedly splitting already
narrow dimensions is discouraged. To keep this optimisation affordable, LECT
adopts a depth-synchronous split schedule: once the best dimension $d_h$ has
been selected for depth $h$, every node at that same depth is split along
$d_h$. This amortises split probing, regularises worker partitions, and keeps
the tree geometrically coherent across subproblems. The resulting KD tree is
therefore not merely a geometric
partition of $\Q$; it is a certificate-aware refinement tree whose topology is
chosen to reduce downstream envelope slack as aggressively as possible.

\subsection{Parent-Envelope Refinement Across Depth}
\label{sec:lect_parent_refine}

Directly computing an envelope on a large interval is often loose. After a node
has been split, its two children carry strictly more local information, so the
parent's logical envelope can be refined using child information instead of
remaining tied to the original coarse certificate. This is the second
multiresolution principle behind LECT: refinement should improve not only the
children themselves, but also the representation of the region they came from.

\begin{proposition}[Child-union parent refinement]
\label{prop:parent_union}
Let a parent interval satisfy $I = I_L \cup I_R$, where $I_L$ and $I_R$ are the
two child intervals created by a binary split. If $B_\ell^L$ and $B_\ell^R$ are
conservative link envelopes for link $\ell$ over $I_L$ and $I_R$, respectively,
then
\[
\widetilde{B}_\ell(I) := B_\ell^L \cup B_\ell^R
\]
is a conservative envelope for link $\ell$ over the full parent interval $I$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The swept link set over the parent interval decomposes as
\[
\bigcup_{q\in I} L_\ell(q)
= \left(\bigcup_{q\in I_L} L_\ell(q)\right)
\cup
\left(\bigcup_{q\in I_R} L_\ell(q)\right).
\]
By assumption, the first term is contained in $B_\ell^L$ and the second in
$B_\ell^R$. Their union therefore contains the full swept set over $I$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:parent_union} gives the ideal parent-update rule: once a node has
been split, the union of child envelopes is a sound refined representation of
the parent. The gain can be substantial, but it depends on the representation.
If the parent is allowed to store a union-like object, such as a sub-box family
or a sparse grid, the refinement is retained. In an AABB-based cache, however,
the most storage-efficient parent payload is usually the enclosing AABB of the
two child AABBs, which is compact but inevitably reintroduces some geometric
inflation. In a grid-based cache, by contrast, parent refinement is simply the
set-theoretic union of the two child voxel maps, implemented as brickwise OR
with no further shape loss beyond the voxelisation itself. The natural design
conclusion is therefore representation-specific: AABB payloads prioritise a
compact persistent witness, while grid payloads preserve the exact multiscale
union structure. A parent node may keep a coarse index envelope for fast
traversal, but its geometric witness should be the union-refined
payload inherited from its children whenever the chosen representation supports
that operation faithfully. This is what makes LECT genuinely multiresolution:
refinement improves not only leaf resolution, but also the persistent geometric
quality of previously visited coarse regions.

\subsection{Storage Structure and Persistence}\label{sec:lect_storage}

At the systems level, LECT is best organised as a flat node pool indexed by a
global node id space. Parallel arrays store interval bounds, child links,
canonical symmetry keys, endpoint-envelope payloads, optional persistent
grid-link payloads, parent-refinement payloads, occupancy counters, and
residency flags. This layout provides
$\mathcal{O}(1)$ random access, contiguous scans over envelope arrays, and a
clean separation between immutable geometric evidence and mutable planner-side
state. It also supports cheap worker snapshots: local trees can share the same
historical geometric base while adding only their newly materialised frontier,
after which newly created nodes are transplanted back into the master cache.
The storage policy should follow reconstruction cost. Endpoint envelopes are the
fundamental persistent record. Grid link envelopes are also worth storing,
because voxelisation and brick construction are the expensive part and because
grid unions can be reused exactly across depth. By contrast, AABB envelopes do
not need their subdivided variants to be stored as first-class payloads: once
endpoint envelopes are cached, the AABB envelope for any chosen subdivision
level $S$ can be reconstructed very quickly by interpolation and
coordinate-wise padding.

\paragraph{Grid payload layout.}
Grid link envelopes are most naturally stored as sparse voxel bitmasks. In the
current design, workspace voxels are grouped into $8^3=512$-voxel BitBricks, so
each non-empty brick occupies 64 bytes and is indexed by its integer brick
coordinate. A grid envelope payload is therefore a sparse map from brick index
to brick word array. Collision checking reduces to wordwise bitwise-AND against
a pre-rasterised obstacle grid, while parent refinement and sibling merges are
implemented as brickwise bitwise-OR. This representation is especially valuable
inside LECT because the union of child grids is itself the exact parent payload
at the chosen voxel resolution, so no extra inflation is introduced during
multiresolution refinement beyond the voxelisation error already present.

\paragraph{Symmetry compression.}
For manipulators whose base joint exhibits a four-sector rotational symmetry, a
node key can be canonicalised to one representative $q_0$ sector and the stored
envelope rotated back to the queried sector at access time. Such a $Z_4$
symmetry layer is structurally compatible with LECT because the cache key is
already an interval in joint space, and it reduces duplicate storage for
repeatedly visited symmetric regions. A natural refinement is to align splits
along $q_0$ with the nearest sector boundary $k\pi/2$ rather than with the raw
geometric midpoint, so that neighbouring nodes map to the same canonical cache
sector whenever possible. In this way symmetry is not merely a compression
trick; it actively shapes the tree toward higher cross-query reuse.

\paragraph{Persistence and residency management.}
LECT is intended to be lifelong across independent processes, so its natural
storage model is a persistent binary image with memory-mapped access,
on-demand node residency, and incremental flushing of modified nodes. A clean
design splits the node pool into a writable in-memory frontier $V$ and a
read-only mapped history $M$ identified by a robot-kinematics fingerprint.
Reads query $V$ first and then fall back to $M$ in zero-copy fashion; writes
materialise only the touched mapped nodes into $V$. On flush, nodes newly added
since the last persisted generation are appended in binary order, and the file
header is updated through a crash-atomic rename so that cached evidence grows
monotonically across runs. Under this design, cold nodes remain on disk, hot
nodes stay resident in RAM, and later sessions inherit previously materialised
geometric evidence immediately after startup. The result is a cache that is
lazy in memory, persistent on disk, symmetry-aware in its key space, and
explicitly engineered for cross-scene and cross-process reuse.

The previous chapter described the scene-independent certificate structure used
by \sbf{}. The role of this chapter is complementary: given a fixed obstacle
set $\calO$ and a set of task-relevant seeds, we construct an
obstacle-specific forest of conservative boxes together with the adjacency graph
used later for planning queries. The construction layer therefore consumes the
reusable evidence stored in LECT and turns it into a scene-level free-space
representation.

Conceptually, the build process has three stages. First, a seed-guided descent
in LECT discovers conservative boxes around selected configurations. Second, these
boxes are expanded into a forest under a strict face-adjacency rule so that the
result remains graph-searchable as a corridor representation rather than only a
bag of disconnected conservative regions. Third, the forest is consolidated by
promotion and coarsening before it is exported as a face-adjacency graph. The
chapter is organised around exactly these three operations.

\subsection{Construction Objective}\label{sec:forest_objective}

Let $\Q$ denote the joint-limit box and let $\calO$ denote the current obstacle
set. The construction objective is to build a finite family
\[
\mathcal{F} = \{B_1,\dots,B_N\}, \qquad B_i \subseteq \Q,
\]
such that every $B_i$ is a validated free-space box, the union
$\bigcup_i B_i$ covers as much useful query-relevant free space as possible
under the available box, time, and depth budgets, and the induced adjacency
graph supports efficient downstream search. This object is scene-specific:
changing $\calO$ changes which intervals are accepted into the forest, even
though the underlying envelope evidence in LECT remains reusable.

The central structural rule is that the forest is not merely a free-space cover,
but a \emph{corridor-compatible} free-space cover. Boxes are therefore inserted
under a face-adjacency constraint.

\begin{definition}[Face-adjacent boxes]\label{def:face_adjacent}
Let
\[
B_i = \prod_{d=1}^{n}[l_i^{(d)},u_i^{(d)}],
\qquad
B_j = \prod_{d=1}^{n}[l_j^{(d)},u_j^{(d)}].
\]
The boxes $B_i$ and $B_j$ are face-adjacent if there exists exactly one
dimension $d^\star$ such that the two intervals are flush in that dimension,
and the intervals overlap with positive measure in every other dimension. In
other words, the boxes share an $(n-1)$-dimensional face rather than only a
lower-dimensional boundary contact.
\end{definition}

This restriction is geometrically important. It excludes pinched contacts that
are formally connected as sets but unusable as stable corridor primitives. The
output of the construction stage is therefore a forest of conservative boxes whose
connectivity is already aligned with the path-planning role they must later
serve.

\subsection{Find Free Box}\label{sec:forest_ffb}

The most atomic construction primitive is the find-free-box procedure (pseudocode
labelled ``Find Free Box''). Given a seed configuration $q\in\Q$, this
procedure descends the LECT from the root toward the leaf interval that
contains $q$. Along this descent, endpoint and link envelopes are
materialised lazily whenever they are needed, and occupancy metadata are
consulted so that already saturated subtrees are skipped early. If the current
node is validated collision-free, it is returned immediately as a box candidate;
otherwise the node is refined along the depth-synchronous split schedule of
\Cref{sec:lect_split}, and the descent continues into the child containing $q$.

The procedure therefore consumes the scene-independent cache in exactly the way
it was designed to be used: the tree topology and endpoint payloads are reused,
while the scene-dependent question ``is this interval free under $\calO$?'' is
answered only when the grower actually visits that interval. FFB is also the
place where the two forms of laziness meet. Tree refinement is lazy because a
node is split only when a seed attempts to pass through it, and envelope
materialisation is lazy because link-level payloads are instantiated only when
their collision-refutation value is needed. The corresponding construction
primitive is summarised in \Cref{alg:ffb_rewrite}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Find Free Box}
\label{alg:ffb_rewrite}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Seed configuration $q$, LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$, depth cap $D_{\max}$
\ENSURE Conservative box $B$ or failure
\STATE $v \gets \mathrm{root}(T)$; $d \gets 0$
\WHILE{$d \le D_{\max}$}
  \STATE materialise the endpoint and link envelopes of $v$ if needed
  \IF{$v$ is marked fully occupied}
    \STATE \textbf{return} failure
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$v$ is validated collision-free with respect to $\calO$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} interval $I_v$
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$d = D_{\max}$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} failure
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$v$ has no children}
    \STATE split $v$ along the depth-synchronous split dimension of depth $d$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $v \gets$ the child of $v$ whose interval contains $q$; $d \gets d+1$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{return} failure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Termination and conservative output of the find-free-box procedure]
\label{prop:ffb_rewrite}
Let the LECT depth cap be $D_{\max}$. The find-free-box procedure terminates
after at most $D_{\max}+1$ descent or refinement decisions. Moreover, every
interval returned by the procedure is a conservative box, i.e., every
configuration in that interval is collision-free with respect to $\calO$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each step of the find-free-box procedure either returns immediately or moves
one level deeper into the tree, so the number of possible descent decisions
is bounded by the depth cap. If an interval is returned, its link envelopes
have been verified disjoint from every obstacle in $\calO$. By the envelope
construction of \Cref{sec:link_envelopes}, those envelopes conservatively
contain the swept link geometry over the whole interval, so every configuration
in the interval is collision-free.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Forest Growth}\label{sec:forest_growth}

Starting from an initial seed set, the forest is grown by repeatedly invoking
the find-free-box procedure on carefully chosen candidate configurations.
Each seed first produces a root box through the find-free-box procedure, and
these root boxes initialise one or more connected components of the forest.
The main grower should be understood as an
evidence-guided expansion process rather than as a generic random cover:
every iteration chooses a frontier component, proposes a nearby interval worth
testing, and accepts the result only when it extends the forest by a usable
face-adjacent corridor segment.

The two key decisions in each iteration are therefore where to expand and how
to turn that decision into a conservative box. A natural policy is coordinated
multi-tree growth: components that are still small or still disconnected
receive higher priority, because their expansion contributes most to global
corridor formation. Once a frontier box has been selected, a new candidate seed
is generated by sampling in configuration space, identifying the nearest
conservative box, and projecting the sample toward a boundary location that
  encourages face-adjacent expansion. The find-free-box procedure is then called on this projected seed.
  The candidate is accepted into the forest only if it is validated by the
  find-free-box procedure and preserves the face-adjacency structure of
  \Cref{def:face_adjacent}. When
components stall, the same loop shifts the sampling distribution toward
  cross-component bridge regions so that later find-free-box calls concentrate near the
missing corridor links. The resulting method is summarised in
\Cref{alg:forest_growth}.

In addition to uniform and goal-biased sampling, the grower uses an
``unexplored-region'' sampling mode. With probability $p_u$ (default
$\sbfScanUnexplored$ in the paper configuration) a candidate sample is drawn by
performing a free-volume-weighted walk down the current LECT: leaves with
larger unoccupied free volume are more likely to be selected. This biases FFB
toward under-explored parts of configuration space, improving coverage
efficiency and reducing the need for costly cross-component bridging. The
mechanism is compatible with both coordinated multi-tree growth and serial
RRT-like expansion.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Forest Growth}
\label{alg:forest_growth}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$, seed set $\mathcal{S}$, box budget $N_{\max}$, time budget $t_{\max}$
\ENSURE Conservative forest $\mathcal{F}$
\STATE $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset$; initialise connected components from $\mathcal{S}$
  \FORALL{$q \in \mathcal{S}$}
  \STATE $B \gets \text{FindFreeBox}(q, T, \calO)$
  \IF{$B \neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE insert $B$ into $\mathcal{F}$ as a root box
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\WHILE{$|\mathcal{F}| < N_{\max}$ and time budget not exhausted}
  \STATE choose a frontier component with priority to disconnected or undersized components
  \STATE draw a candidate sample $q_{\mathrm{cand}}$ from the current growth distribution
  \STATE find the nearest conservative frontier box $B_{\mathrm{near}}$
  \STATE project $q_{\mathrm{cand}}$ toward a face of $B_{\mathrm{near}}$ to obtain $q_{\mathrm{seed}}$
  \STATE $B_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \text{FindFreeBox}(q_{\mathrm{seed}}, T, \calO)$
  \IF{$B_{\mathrm{new}} \neq \emptyset$ and $B_{\mathrm{new}}$ is face-adjacent to $B_{\mathrm{near}}$}
    \STATE insert $B_{\mathrm{new}}$ into $\mathcal{F}$ and update component connectivity
  \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \RETURN $\mathcal{F}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The current paper configuration uses coordinated multi-goal growth as the fast
path. Five seed configurations initialise the shelf roots, while the grower and
bridge worker pools are both fixed to the same 8-core/8-thread budget. The
default disables the up-front seed bridge, keeps rescue bridging available for
queries that still need repair, and disables the optional coarsening pass in the
fastest full-build setting. This configuration keeps the growth schedule simple
enough to preserve one authoritative forest state, while the LECT still supplies
reusable kinematic evidence across threads and across cache-warm reruns. An
experimental partitioned LECT grower is available as an engineering variant, but
it is not the default used for the shelf-scene results reported below.

One boundary must remain explicit. A point-level bridge may still be used later
as an end-to-end fallback when the box corridor remains disconnected,
but such a point path is not part of the conservative forest constructed here. The
output of the present chapter is the box-level corridor object only.

\subsection{Forest Consolidation}\label{sec:forest_consolidation}

Raw growth tends to produce a forest that is finer than necessary. The purpose
of consolidation is therefore to replace a large collection of locally valid
boxes by a smaller collection of equally conservative but more useful regions.
Two operations are central.

The first is \emph{promotion}. When two occupied sibling leaves are both
collision-free and their parent interval is also collision-free, the two leaves can be
replaced by the parent. This is the most local form of coarsening and directly
reuses the multiresolution structure of LECT.

The second is \emph{multi-level coarsening}. Exact face-contact merges are the
cleanest case: if two boxes share a full face and their union is itself one box,
the merged region remains conservative by construction. More general adjacency
merges are handled by constructing a candidate parent region and accepting it
only when its chosen envelope representation is still conservative against the
current obstacle set. This is where the representation choices of
\Cref{sec:link_envelopes,sec:lect} become operational. In an AABB-based forest, the compact parent witness
is typically the enclosing AABB of the two child boxes. In a grid-based forest,
the parent witness is the exact voxel union of the two child payloads. The
former is cheaper to store, while the latter preserves geometric detail more
faithfully across depth. The consolidation logic is summarised in
\Cref{alg:forest_consolidation}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Forest Consolidation}
\label{alg:forest_consolidation}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Conservative forest $\mathcal{F}$, LECT $T$, obstacle set $\calO$
\ENSURE Consolidated conservative forest $\mathcal{F}'$
\STATE $\mathcal{F}' \gets \mathcal{F}$
\STATE repeatedly promote collision-free sibling leaves to their collision-free parent whenever possible
\STATE merge exact face-contact pairs whose union is itself a single box
\FORALL{adjacent candidate pairs $(B_i,B_j)$ in $\mathcal{F}'$}
  \STATE build a candidate parent region $H$ and score its volume expansion
  \IF{the chosen envelope representation of $H$ is conservatively collision-free with respect to $\calO$}
    \STATE replace $B_i,B_j$ by $H$
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE remove redundant boxes that are fully contained in larger conservative regions
\STATE \RETURN $\mathcal{F}'$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Sound forest consolidation]
\label{thm:forest_consolidation}
Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a forest of conservative boxes. Suppose that every promotion
or coarsening step replaces a set of boxes by a new region $H$ only when the
selected envelope representation of $H$ is conservatively collision-
free with respect to $\calO$. Then the resulting forest
$\mathcal{F}'$ is still a forest of conservative boxes and satisfies
\[
\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{F}} B \subseteq \bigcup_{B'\in\mathcal{F}'} B'.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every accepted parent or merged region contains the boxes it replaces, so the
union of the forest cannot shrink. By assumption, each replacement is accepted
only after a conservative envelope check has shown that the new region is
collision-free. Therefore every region in $\mathcal{F}'$ remains conservative.
\end{proof}

The purpose of this theorem is not merely correctness bookkeeping. It clarifies
why consolidation belongs in the main method narrative: the forest is not
post-processed by an arbitrary geometry simplifier, but by a second conservative
construction stage whose accepted outputs remain valid planning regions. In
that sense, consolidation is not an optional cleanup pass; it is the final
certificate-preserving reduction that turns a raw growth trace into a compact,
query-ready corridor structure.

\subsection{Corridor Query}
\label{sec:forest_graph}

After growth and consolidation, the forest is exported as a graph
$G=(V,E)$ with one vertex per conservative box. Two vertices are connected by an
edge when their boxes are face-adjacent in the sense of
\Cref{def:face_adjacent}. The resulting graph is therefore not an auxiliary
data structure added after the fact; it is the direct combinatorial shadow of
the conservative corridor structure enforced throughout the construction process.

This pair $(\mathcal{F},G)$ is the final planning object produced by the build
stage. Querying it is intentionally lightweight. Given start and goal
configurations $q_s,q_g$, the planner associates them with start-side and
goal-side boxes in $\mathcal{F}$ and then searches $G$ for a connecting box
sequence. The query problem is therefore reduced to box-corridor
retrieval inside an already constructed free-space representation.

A shortest path on $G$ yields a box sequence whose union defines a conservative
corridor between the two query endpoints. A geometric path is then recovered as
a waypoint chain inside that corridor. The optional post-processing in
\Cref{alg:pathopt_rewrite} performs only corridor-constrained waypoint
elimination and refinement. This step is purely local; it introduces no new
global planning problem, and feasibility is inherited from the conservative
corridor itself.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Lightweight corridor cleanup (\textsc{PathOpt})}
\label{alg:pathopt_rewrite}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textit{// Connectivity-preserving waypoint elimination}
\REPEAT
  \STATE $\mathit{changed} \gets \textsc{false}$; $i \gets 1$
  \WHILE{$i \le K-2$}
    \IF{$\overline{c_i c_{i+2}} \subset \mathcal{U}$}
      \STATE delete $c_{i+1}$; $K \gets K-1$; $\mathit{changed} \gets \textsc{true}$
    \ELSE
      \STATE $i \gets i+1$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDWHILE
\UNTIL{\textbf{not} $\mathit{changed}$}
\STATE \textit{// Corridor-constrained waypoint refinement}
\FOR{$i = 2$ \textbf{to} $K-1$}
  \STATE $c_i^\star \gets \tfrac{1}{2}(c_{i-1}+c_{i+1})$
  \STATE \textbf{if} $c_i^\star \in \mathcal{U}$ \textbf{then} $c_i \gets c_i^\star$
  \STATE \textbf{else} project $c_i^\star$ to $\mathcal{U}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN $\langle c_1,\dots,c_K\rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

One reporting boundary remains essential. If no conservative corridor can be
recovered, a point-level fallback may still be invoked to produce an end-to-end
solution. Such a path is useful operationally, but it is not part of the
reusable conservative corridor object constructed here. \Cref{sec:forest} therefore closes
with the conservative forest together with the minimal corridor-query mechanism
required to consume it, while \cref{sec:exp} evaluates build cost, query
cost, path quality, and fallback frequency without enlarging the
methodological claim.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Soundness}\label{sec:exp_setup_sound}

The experiment section follows the same logic as the method. We first justify
the selected endpoint and link-envelope pipelines, and then evaluate the full
planning system on the target multi-query workload together with robustness and
sensitivity evidence. Unless otherwise noted, we report success rate (SR), wall
time, path length, and fallback frequency over repeated random seeds.

The shelf-scene benchmark is treated as one combined 16-obstacle workload.
The five query pairs are not independent scenes; they are canonical queries in
one shared reusable build.
Whenever a final feasible path requires point-level fallback, we report this
explicitly instead of merging it into box-corridor success. Such paths remain
valid end-to-end outputs but indicate incomplete conservative connectivity.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fairness Protocol}
\label{sec:exp_repro}

All retained rows follow one fixed protocol:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item same thread budget and fixed CPU-core set across methods/reruns;
  \item no concurrent experiment groups (avoid contention);
  \item same seed lists and aggregation rules;
  \item explicit build/query timing separation.
\end{itemize}
This protocol is used in the shelf-scene and cross-robot tables, including
warm-versus-cold replay comparisons. The scope of empirical claims and the
relation to released scripts are discussed in \cref{sec:discussion}.

The second stage then sweeps only HullGrid across the voxel-size schedule.
At $\delta=0.04$ m, HullGrid tightens further to 67.3\% at 24.1~$\mu$s with

\Cref{tab:link_envelope_pipeline} also reports cache-side diagnostics from a
controlled shelf-scene replay. In every compared run, LECT starts empty, the
tree-snapshot cache is disabled, and the cold-fill row disables EP pre-probe
while writing the EP/Grid entries later consumed by the validated cache-hit
replay. A cache-hit row is retained only when the build profile reports zero
EP/Grid misses and zero grid-compute fallbacks. These columns therefore measure
geometric-evidence reuse on a matched replay route rather than the much larger
effect of loading a pre-expanded tree.

In that table, $t_{eval}$ denotes a standalone local microkernel time in $\mu$s:
the script measures endpoint-envelope evaluation, then the chosen link-envelope
construction, and reports their sum. This deliberately excludes grower-side
parent-FK reuse and route-level cache effects. The column $t_{read}$ denotes
LECT envelope-read time per cache hit in $\mu\mathrm{s}/\mathrm{hit}$,
estimated from the cache-hit build profile as envelope-expansion time divided by
the sum of endpoint and grid cache-hit counts on the retained rows. Unlike
$t_{eval}$, $t_{read}$ is keyed only by envelope representation ($S$ and
$\delta$), not by endpoint source, so rows such as Crit+HullGrid and
IFK+HullGrid at the same $\delta$ share one cache-read value. The D32 column is
estimated from a separate 8-thread LECT-envelope fill microbenchmark that drives
a depth-synchronous split sequence, reuses incremental FK, and computes the
selected endpoint source plus link envelope. We extrapolate
$T_{D32}=N_{32}/r_{\mathrm{fill}}$ with $N_{32}=2^{33}-1$ and the measured fill
throughput $r_{\mathrm{fill}}$, so D32 represents the parallel fill path rather
than a one-shot $t_{eval}$ multiplication. The disk column uses a compact
base-node record for LinkIAABB and adds a sparse-grid payload only for grid
variants. Read together, the cache-side columns support the same design
conclusion as the runtime columns: LinkIAABB subdivision changes per-node
footprint only mildly because endpoint evidence is shared, whereas
moderate-resolution grids trade higher read and storage cost for tighter
envelopes. In the current implementation, CritSample also follows the global
incremental-FK reuse path whenever parent FK state and changed-dimension context
are available, so moderate-resolution grids remain targeted tools rather than
blanket defaults.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The SafeBoxForest system makes several deliberate design choices that reflect
specific operating points and trade-offs. This section clarifies those choices
and their implications.

\subsection{Generality Across Scene, Robot, and Dimension}\label{sec:exp_generality}

\paragraph{Geometric and modelling scope.}
The theoretical reductions emphasise capsule-like links and convex outer bounds;
sharp polyhedral geometry without an offset representation inherits conservative
but potentially loose envelopes (\cref{sec:link_envelopes}). Axis-aligned
configuration-space boxes trade expressive region shape for cheap certification,
adjacency tests, and cache-friendly replay; they are not chosen to minimise
per-query path length against heavy nonconvex-region optimisers on a single
instance. These are deliberate design points rather than implementation gaps.

\paragraph{Empirical generality.}
All cross-method comparisons are tied to the protocol in
\cref{sec:exp_repro}: fixed thread and core affinities, no concurrent
experiment groups, common seed sets, and explicit build versus query timers.
Generality to new robots, scene distributions, or collision engines is
supported only insofar as the same invariants (conservative envelopes, valid
obstacle models) hold; the cross-robot results in
\cref{tab:panda,fig:exp5_cross_robot_baselines} are one step in that
direction, not a claim of domain independence.

\paragraph{Seed sensitivity and initialization burden.}
Under the retained shelf-scene and cross-robot protocols in
\cref{tab:query,tab:panda}, IRIS-style pipelines are more sensitive to the
quality of initial seeds and usually benefit from careful manual curation.
SBF is also seed-driven, but its RRT-like multi-seed growth is less brittle:
when a subset of seeds is poor, expansion can continue from other random seeds
and still form usable box corridors. This robustness should be interpreted
as an empirical property of the present protocol rather than a universal
guarantee across all scene families.

\paragraph{Build ablation interpretation.}
The build-ablation discussion shows that removing seed bridges or coarsening
changes time allocation and box counts on the combined shelf scene, while
partitioned or legacy-parallel grow variants substantially shift wall-clock
toward parallel bridge phases; baseline routing preserves SR under the reported
seeds, but the result should be read as component attribution on one workload,
not as a guarantee under every scene family.

\paragraph{Artifact and reproducibility.}
The accompanying implementation and numbered experiment drivers used to produce
the generated tables can be rerun under the same flags to regenerate timing and
aggregate statistics; configuration paths and aggregation scripts are written to
match the definitions above.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \textsc{SafeBoxForest} (\sbf{}), a conservative multi-query
planning framework that combines interval-envelope certification, a scene-independent
Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree (LECT), and adaptive RRT-like free-box growth.
By decoupling robot-kinematics evidence from scene-specific obstacle filtering,
SBF reuses certified geometric structure across repeated queries and scene edits.
On the shelf-scene workload, compared with IRIS under the same protocol, SBF
achieves substantially lower reusable build cost with comparable online query
latency and moderately longer paths. On randomized cross-robot scenes, the same
build-time advantage remains, while path quality and fallback frequency are more
sensitive to scene topology.

\sbf{} is best suited to long-horizon or batch manipulation regimes with stable
robot kinematics and repeated query structure. In this setting, LECT persistence
and reusable box-corridor planning provide practical amortization benefits. The
limits of axis-aligned covers, capsule-centric envelopes, and workload-scoped
evaluation are summarized in \Cref{sec:discussion}.

Future work will focus on improving residual connectivity in narrow passages, generalizing
cache and forest update rules to accommodate highly dynamic environments, and maintaining
certificates under incremental scene changes.
