\title{SafeBoxForest: Certified Box Covers and Lifelong Envelope Caching\\
for Multi-Query Manipulator Planning}

\author{TIAN,~Yu and Ren,~Hongliang%
\thanks{The authors are with the Department of Electronic Engineering,
The Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK), Hong Kong SAR, China.
E-mail: \texttt{yutian@link.cuhk.edu.hk}.}}

\begin{abstract}
This paper presents \textsc{SafeBoxForest}~(SBF), a certified box-cover and
multi-query planning framework for high-degree-of-freedom serial manipulators.
The central idea is to cache reusable geometric certificates at the level of
joint-space intervals rather than individual configurations. SBF organises this
evidence in a Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree~(LECT), a kinematics-keyed KD tree
whose nodes store conservative endpoint and link envelopes that can be reused
across queries and across obstacle layouts as long as the robot kinematics do
not change. The certification chain factors safety into endpoint \iAABB,
LinkIAABB, and certified-box tests; in the current implementation the cache stores
zero-radius centreline geometry and applies per-link radii only at
collision-query time, preserving a scene-independent envelope representation.
Built on top of LECT, SBF grows a forest of certified configuration-space
boxes, constructs a face-adjacency graph, and answers queries by shortest-path
search plus lightweight path post-processing. This design targets workloads in
which many related manipulation queries must be solved in the same scene, or in
which scenes evolve while the robot model stays fixed. In this draft, the
empirical section is organised around soundness, envelope-pipeline trade-offs,
end-to-end reusable-query performance, generality, and sensitivity. The core
current v6 Marcucci reruns show the intended regime clearly: one reusable box
forest is built once, then serves all five canonical cabinet queries with
cached-query latencies reported separately from the reusable build. Compared
with one-shot sampling baselines and expensive implicit-set covers, SBF is
positioned as a system that prioritises fast reusable cover construction and
explicit certificate reuse, while any point-level bridge fallback is reported
separately from the certified box corridor itself.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Motion planning, configuration space, certified box cover, interval arithmetic,
envelope caching, multi-query planning, serial manipulator.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Sampling-based planning remains the practical default for high-DOF robot
manipulators because it can work with a black-box collision predicate and scales
to complicated scenes~\cite{lavalle2006planning,lavalle1998rapidly,
kavraki1996probabilistic,karaman2011sampling}. Its weakness is equally clear:
the online cost is dominated by repeated collision checking and by the local,
query-by-query nature of graph expansion. Region-based planners and
Graph-of-Convex-Sets~(GCS) methods attack this bottleneck from the opposite
direction by moving more work offline and planning over certified free-space
regions~\cite{deits2015computing,marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}.
However, once region quality becomes the main determinant of performance, the
real systems question is no longer only how to search for one path, but how to
accumulate, certify, and reuse geometric evidence about free space across a
stream of related queries.

That question is especially important in long-horizon manipulation settings such
as shelving, pick-and-place, and human-shared workcells. In these workloads the
robot kinematics are fixed, but obstacles and task endpoints vary over time. A
planner that rebuilds all geometric evidence from scratch after every scene
change wastes the strongest available invariant: the mapping from a
configuration-space interval to a conservative workspace envelope depends on the
robot model, not on a particular obstacle set. This observation suggests a
different decomposition of the planning problem: separate the reusable,
kinematics-driven certificate from the scene-specific free-space cover.

\textsc{SafeBoxForest} is built around that decomposition. Its core data
structure is a Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree~(LECT), a kinematics-keyed cache of
endpoint and link envelopes over joint-space boxes. In this paper,
\emph{lifelong} is used in an operational sense: the cached geometric evidence
is persistent across processes and obstacle scenes and is invalidated only when
the robot kinematics change. The certification pipeline follows a simple chain:
interval forward kinematics maps a joint-space box to endpoint \iAABB s,
adjacent endpoint boxes define conservative link envelopes, and a configuration-
space box is accepted only when all of its link envelopes are disjoint from all
workspace obstacles. The current implementation stores zero-radius centreline
geometry in the cache and applies link radii only at collision time, so the
cached object remains scene-independent and reusable.

Built on top of LECT, SBF grows an obstacle-specific forest of certified
configuration-space boxes, constructs a face-adjacency graph, and answers
queries by box localisation, graph search, and lightweight path refinement.
This design preserves a clean separation between reusable geometric evidence and
scene-level planning state. It also forces an important reporting discipline:
the certified box corridor and any point-level fallback are not conflated. In
the current code path, IFK-derived envelopes are the certified hot path stored
in the main cache, while any point-level bridge used to repair a disconnected
corridor is treated as an end-to-end fallback and reported explicitly rather
than folded into the certificate.

The resulting objective differs from that of both one-shot samplers and pure
convex-cover planners. SBF does not try to produce the globally tightest region
set per scene, nor does it optimise a single query in isolation. Instead, it
aims to make certified free-space evidence cheap to reuse, cheap to update, and
cheap to consume by downstream planners. Under that objective, the relevant
performance questions are offline build cost, online query latency on a reused
cover, cross-scene robustness, and the extent to which the certificate remains
useful after the scene changes.

\textbf{The main contributions are as follows.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
	\item \emph{A certificate-and-cache formulation for manipulator planning.}
	We cast certified planning around a reusable three-stage chain---endpoint
		\iAABB, link envelope, and certified box---and make explicit the implementation
		invariant that cached geometry is zero-radius while link radii are applied at
		query time.

	\item \emph{A Lifelong Envelope Cache Tree.}
	We organise interval-level kinematic evidence in a persistent, kinematics-
	keyed KD tree that supports lazy materialisation and reuse across queries and
	obstacle layouts, rather than caching only paths or single configurations.

	\item \emph{A certified box-forest planning pipeline.}
	We build a face-adjacent forest of certified configuration-space boxes and use
	it as the backbone for multi-query planning by graph search and lightweight
	post-processing, while keeping any non-certified fallback outside the main
	certificate.

	\item \emph{A full empirical study template for the current implementation.}
	We organise the evaluation around soundness, envelope-pipeline trade-offs,
	offline build cost, cached online query performance, baseline comparisons,
	and cross-scene / cross-robot behaviour. The core cabinet SBF query and
	envelope/cache tables are populated from recent reruns, while the
	longer stress, baseline, and sensitivity slots remain explicitly marked where
	data are still pending.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is therefore positioned around a specific claim: reusable certified
geometric evidence is a planning primitive in its own right. SBF is one way to
realise that primitive for serial manipulators by combining interval-style
conservatism, cached link-envelope evidence, and a box-graph query pipeline.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Convex Region Planning and Graphs of Convex Sets}

Convex region planners attack motion planning by constructing a certified cover
of free space and then solving graph search or convex optimisation on top of
that cover. IRIS introduced the now-standard idea of computing large
collision-free convex regions by alternating separating hyperplanes and region
inflation~\cite{deits2015computing}. Later work extended the same line into
configuration space, improved region quality, or improved graph compactness
through visibility-graph and clique-cover constructions~\cite{amice2022finding,
petersen2023growing,werner2024approximating}. Marcucci \etal{} made the
Graph-of-Convex-Sets formulation particularly influential by showing that motion
planning can be cast as optimisation over a graph of certified convex
regions~\cite{marcucci2023motion,marcucci2024shortest}.

SBF is aligned with this family in spirit but not in geometry. Its free-space
regions are axis-aligned configuration-space boxes rather than general
polytopes. This choice is intentionally biased toward cheap certification,
cheap adjacency tests, and persistent cache reuse. The resulting cover is
coarser than a high-quality convex decomposition, but it is also substantially
cheaper to update and to reuse in multi-query workloads. In that sense SBF is
best viewed not as a replacement for convex-region planning, but as an
alternative front end that emphasises reusable certificate throughput over per-
region geometric expressiveness.

\subsection{Certified Envelopes, Interval Methods, and Collision Checking}

Interval analysis provides a natural foundation for conservative geometric
certification because nonlinear image sets can be enclosed without losing
soundness~\cite{moore2009introduction,jaulin2001applied}. In robotics, interval
methods have long been used for kinematics and certified workspace
analysis~\cite{merlet2004solving,merlet2006interval}. SBF follows that
tradition, but shifts the unit of certification from a workspace reachability
set or a single configuration to a reusable configuration-space box. The key
object is therefore not a precise workspace mesh or a narrow-phase contact
query, but a conservative link envelope that can be cached and replayed across
many planning calls.

Classical collision libraries solve a different problem. Hierarchies such as
OBB trees and libraries such as FCL are effective for single-configuration
collision detection and proximity queries~\cite{gottschalk1996obbtree,
pan2012fcl,gilbert1988fast}. They do not, however, provide a scene-independent
cache of certified interval-level evidence. SBF is complementary to this stack:
it uses conservative envelopes to avoid repeated narrow-phase work on already
certified boxes, and reserves point-level collision checking for the remaining
hard cases.

\subsection{Multi-Query Reuse and Configuration-Space Decomposition}

Multi-query planners typically amortise effort by reusing a graph structure such
as a roadmap. PRM is the classical example: build a connectivity graph once and
reuse it for many start-goal pairs~\cite{kavraki1996probabilistic,
bohlin2000path}. Later lazy-search and certificate-based variants reduce online
edge evaluation or cache known-free configurations~\cite{dellin2016unifying,
schmerling2015optimal}. Experience-based systems likewise reuse prior search
results, usually in the form of trajectories or local connections rather than a
certified region decomposition~\cite{coleman2014moveit}.

SBF operates at a different reuse layer. What is cached is not the path itself,
nor only a collision verdict at one configuration, but the geometric witness
that an entire configuration-space box is safe. This makes its reuse semantics
closer to configuration-space decomposition than to roadmap replay. In spirit,
it also reconnects with classical approximate cell decomposition: free space is
covered by axis-aligned cells whose adjacency can be searched directly.
The difference is that SBF insists on conservative certification and stores the
cells in a persistent, robot-keyed envelope cache rather than treating them as a
throwaway discretisation~\cite{lozanoperez1983spatial,brooks1985subdivision}.

\subsection{Sampling-Based Planning and Trajectory Optimisation}

Sampling-based planners remain the strongest baselines when the goal is to solve
a single query quickly. RRT-Connect is particularly effective in manipulator
planning because it exploits bidirectional growth and often succeeds with very
little global structure~\cite{kuffner2000rrt}. Asymptotically optimal and
informed variants such as RRT*, BIT*, and FMT* improve path quality and search
efficiency when more time is available~\cite{karaman2011sampling,
gammell2015batch,gammell2018informed,janson2015fmt}. OMPL has made these
methods easy to deploy and benchmark in a common software stack~\cite{sucan2012ompl,
moll2015benchmarking}.

Trajectory optimisers address a different stage of the pipeline. CHOMP, STOMP,
TrajOpt, and Gaussian-process motion planners refine an initial path by local
descent or sequential convex optimisation~\cite{ratliff2009chomp,
kalakrishnan2011stomp,schulman2014motion,mukadam2018gpmp2}. SBF uses these
methods as a point of comparison rather than as direct substitutes. Its aim is
to provide a reusable certified backbone on which fast graph search and light
post-processing can run. In that sense SBF is complementary to both one-shot
samplers and local smoothers: it does not eliminate either class of method, but
changes the regime in which they are invoked by supplying reusable region-level
evidence first.

\section{Link Interval Envelopes}\label{sec:link_envelopes}

This section develops the geometric foundation behind the link-envelope layer
used by \sbf{}. The central question is how to outer-bound the swept workspace
volume of a rigid link over a joint interval $I \subseteq \Q$ using a small,
reusable certificate. Our answer is to reduce link envelopes to endpoint
envelopes and a radius-lift step. The reduction is exact for capsule links and
extends as a conservative convex-envelope argument to more general convex link
geometries. The logic of the section is correspondingly simple. We first
separate the constant-radius term for offset bodies such as capsules, then show
that the swept convex envelope of a convex link can be controlled through the
motion of a generating set, and finally instantiate this reduction with the
endpoint and link-envelope representations used by the planner.
